Gratuity in India: Calculation Formula, Tax Exemption & Eligibility Rules

Gratuity is not a bonus your employer chooses to give — it's a statutory right under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972, with a fixed formula and a fixed tax exemption ceiling. Yet most employees discover the details only when they're reading their final settlement letter. Here's the formula, the exemption rules, and the numbers, worked out in full.

Key Takeaways: What You Need to Know First

  • 1. 5-Year Rule: You need 5 years of continuous service to claim gratuity, except in case of death or permanent disablement, where the rule is waived entirely.
  • 2. The Formula: For employees covered under the Act, gratuity = (Last drawn Basic + DA) × 15/26 × number of years of service, with any service period over 6 months rounded up to the next full year.
  • 3. ₹20 Lakh Ceiling: For private-sector employees, tax exemption is capped at ₹20,00,000 under Section 10(10), even though the employer must still pay the full formula amount.
  • 4. Government Employees: Gratuity for Central/State Government employees is 100% tax-exempt with no ceiling at all.
  • 5. Forfeiture Is Narrow: An employer can only forfeit gratuity for proven misconduct causing damage or an offence involving moral turpitude — not for ordinary termination.
  • 6. Section 89 Relief: Any taxable portion above the exemption ceiling can still get partial tax relief by spreading the lump sum across the years it relates to.

Gratuity Is a Right, Not a Gift

The word "gratuity" suggests something optional, almost like a tip. That framing is misleading. For any establishment with 10 or more employees, the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 makes it a statutory obligation on the employer, with a formula the employer cannot unilaterally reduce. Employees frequently underestimate this amount when planning a job change or retirement, partly because it doesn't show up on a monthly payslip the way salary does, and partly because most people only learn the rules when they're already reading a final settlement statement.

Knowing the formula in advance changes two things: it lets you estimate your actual retirement or exit payout years ahead of time, and it tells you exactly how much of that payout will actually reach your bank account after tax.

1. The 5-Year Eligibility Rule (and Its Exceptions)

The baseline rule is simple: you must complete 5 years of continuous service with the same employer to be eligible for gratuity. "Continuous service" includes your notice period, so if your last working day pushes your total tenure past the 5-year mark — even if your original joining date plus 5 years falls a few weeks short — you still qualify.

There are two situations where the 5-year requirement is waived completely:

  • Death of the employee, regardless of how many years were served.
  • Permanent disablement that ends the employee's ability to continue working, regardless of tenure.

In both cases, gratuity is computed on the actual number of years served — even if that's just 8 months — and paid to the nominee or legal heirs.

2. The Exact Calculation Formula

There are two formulas depending on whether your organisation is "covered" under the Payment of Gratuity Act (broadly, any establishment that has ever employed 10 or more people) or falls outside it.

Employer Type Formula Notes
Covered under the Act (Last drawn Basic + DA) × 15/26 × years of service 26 represents assumed working days in a month; 15 is half a month's wages per year of service
Not covered under the Act (Average Basic + DA of last 10 months) ÷ 2 × years of service Uses a calendar month (30 days) basis instead of 26 working days

Rounding rule: Any part of a year of service exceeding 6 months is rounded up to a full year. Someone with 12 years and 7 months of service is treated as having completed 13 years. Someone with 12 years and 4 months is treated as 12 years. This single rounding rule can shift a payout by a full year's worth of gratuity, so it's worth double-checking your relieving date against it.

3. Tax Exemption Under Section 10(10)

Gratuity received is taxed under the head "Income from Salary," but Section 10(10) of the Income Tax Act carves out an exemption that depends on your employer category:

  • Government employees (Central, State, or local authority): fully exempt, no ceiling.
  • Private-sector employees covered under the Act: exemption is the least of (a) actual gratuity received, (b) the statutory ceiling of ₹20,00,000, and (c) the formula amount — 15/26 × last drawn salary × years of service.
  • Private-sector employees not covered under the Act: exemption is the least of (a) actual gratuity received, (b) ₹20,00,000, and (c) half of average salary of the last 10 months × years of service.

Note the distinction here: the ₹20 Lakh figure is a tax exemption ceiling, not a cap on what your employer must pay you. If the formula computes a gratuity higher than ₹20 Lakh, your employer still owes you the full amount — only the portion above ₹20 Lakh becomes taxable in your hands.

4. Two Worked Examples

Example 1: A typical mid-career exit, fully tax-free

An employee at a company covered under the Act resigns after 12 years and 7 months of service. Last drawn Basic + DA is ₹80,000/month.

  • Rounded service period: 13 years (12 years 7 months rounds up)
  • Gratuity = ₹80,000 × 15/26 × 13 = ₹6,00,000

Since ₹6,00,000 is well below the ₹20 Lakh ceiling, the entire amount is exempt. Nothing gets added to taxable salary this year.

Example 2: A senior employee crossing the ₹20 Lakh ceiling

A senior manager retires after 30 years of continuous service at a company covered under the Act. Last drawn Basic + DA is ₹2,50,000/month.

  • Gratuity payable = ₹2,50,000 × 15/26 × 30 = ₹43,26,923 (this is what the employer must actually pay)
  • Exemption = least of (a) ₹43,26,923 actual, (b) ₹20,00,000 statutory ceiling, (c) ₹43,26,923 formula = ₹20,00,000
  • Taxable gratuity = ₹43,26,923 − ₹20,00,000 = ₹23,26,923, added to income from salary and taxed at the applicable slab rate

That taxable slice can still get partial relief. Section 89(1) allows you to recompute tax as if the lump sum had been spread across the years it relates to, which typically softens the blow of a large one-time payment pushing you into a higher effective bracket for that single year. Your employer's payroll or a tax professional can help file Form 10E to claim this relief while submitting your return.

5. When Gratuity Can Be Forfeited

Gratuity is a protected right, and employers cannot withhold it simply because an employee resigned abruptly, performed poorly, or left to join a competitor. The Act permits forfeiture, in whole or in part, only in narrow circumstances:

  • The employee's services were terminated for willfully causing loss, damage, or destruction to the employer's property — and even then, forfeiture is limited to the extent of the actual damage caused.
  • The employee's services were terminated for an act constituting an offence involving moral turpitude, committed during the course of employment, and the termination followed due process.

Ordinary disciplinary termination, performance-based exits, or resignation under pressure do not qualify as grounds for forfeiture. If an employer withholds gratuity outside these narrow grounds, the employee can approach the Controlling Authority under the Act, which has the power to direct payment along with interest for delay.

6. Nomination and Claim Process

Every eligible employee should file Form F with their employer within 30 days of completing one year of service, nominating who should receive gratuity in case of death before the payment is made. Employees with a family at the time of nomination must nominate only family members; a nomination in favour of someone outside the family is invalid if the employee has a family. The form can be updated any time family circumstances change — marriage, birth of a child, and so on.

On separation, the employer is required to determine the gratuity amount and give written notice to the employee (or nominee) specifying the sum payable, and the amount must be paid within 30 days of it becoming due. Delayed payment attracts simple interest, and the employee can file a claim with the Controlling Authority if the employer defaults.

Actionable Next Step: Calculate Your Own Estimate

Take your current Basic + DA and your years of continuous service (rounding any month count over 6 up to the next year), and run the formula: Basic + DA × 15/26 × years. That single number tells you your current estimated gratuity payout if you exited today — a figure worth knowing well before any resignation, negotiation, or retirement conversation.

These figures reflect statutory provisions under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972 and Section 10(10) of the Income Tax Act for FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28); consult a qualified professional for your specific employment terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What happens to my gratuity if I resign before completing 5 years?

You are not entitled to gratuity under the Act if you resign or are terminated (other than for death or disablement) before completing 5 years of continuous service. Some employers pay an ex-gratia amount voluntarily, but there is no statutory right to it.

Does the notice period count toward the 5-year gratuity eligibility?

Yes. Continuous service includes your official last working day, which usually falls at the end of your notice period. If that pushes your total tenure past 5 years, you become eligible even if your joining date plus 5 years alone falls short.

Is gratuity paid to my nominee if I die before completing 5 years?

Yes. The 5-year requirement is waived entirely in case of death or permanent disablement. Gratuity is computed on actual years of service with no minimum, and paid to the nominee registered in Form F, or to legal heirs if no nomination exists.

Is gratuity fully tax-free for government employees?

Yes. Gratuity received by Central Government, State Government, and local authority employees is fully exempt under Section 10(10)(i), with no upper ceiling. The ₹20 Lakh cap applies only to private-sector and other non-government employees.

Can my employer refuse to pay gratuity?

An employer can only forfeit gratuity, wholly or partly, for proven willful damage to company property or an offence involving moral turpitude committed during employment. Ordinary termination or poor performance does not permit forfeiture.

Can I get tax relief if my gratuity pushes me into a higher slab in one year?

Yes. The taxable portion above the exemption limit can qualify for relief under Section 89(1), which recalculates tax as if the amount had been received across the years it relates to, typically reducing the effective tax rate on that lump sum. This requires filing Form 10E.
