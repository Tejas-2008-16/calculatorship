Old vs New Tax Regime in India (FY 2026-27): Complete Comparison Guide

Written by Tejas Waghmare  •  Published: February 2026  •  Last reviewed: 23 November 2026  •  Tax Year: FY 2024-25 & FY 2025-26 (Transitioning to Income-tax Act, 2025 in FY 2026-27)

Educational tax comparison guide only. For individual tax filing decisions, consult a qualified Chartered Accountant. Read our full disclaimer.

Direct Answer: Under the default New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC), resident salaried individuals earning up to ₹12.75 Lakh gross income (after the ₹75,000 standard deduction) pay zero income tax due to the enhanced ₹60,000 Section 87A rebate. For higher salaries, the Old Tax Regime beats the New Regime only if total itemized deductions—combining standard deduction, Section 80C, 80D, HRA, and Section 24(b) home loan interest—exceed roughly ₹5.94 Lakh at ₹15 Lakh salary or ₹8.50 Lakh at ₹25 Lakh+ salary.

A mandatory 4% Health and Education Cess applies to the computed tax under both regimes. Note the critical difference: in the Old Regime, the punishing 30% tax bracket triggers at just ₹10,00,001. Under the New Regime, the 30% bracket starts only above ₹24,00,000 taxable income (with 5%, 10%, 15%, 20%, and 25% intermediate steps).

The FY 2026-27 Breakeven Deduction Table Across Salary Tiers

Your "breakeven deduction" is the total Old Regime deductions (including ₹50,000 standard deduction, Section 80C, 80D, HRA, and home loan interest) required to equal the tax owed under the New Regime. See also our Income-tax Act 2025 section mapping guide:

Worked Numerical Example: ₹15 Lakh CTC Under Both Regimes

Illustrative example, not a real person: Suppose an engineer earns ₹15,00,000 gross salary and under the Old Regime claims ₹50,000 standard deduction + ₹1,80,000 HRA exemption + ₹1,50,000 Section 80C + ₹25,000 Section 80D = ₹4,05,000 total deductions (leaving ₹10,95,000 taxable):

  • Old Tax Regime Total Payable: ₹1,41,000 base + ₹5,640 cess = ₹1,46,640
  • New Tax Regime Total Payable (₹14,25,000 taxable): ₹20,000 (4–8L @ 5%) + ₹40,000 (8–12L @ 10%) + ₹33,750 (12–14.25L @ 15%) = ₹93,750 base + ₹3,750 cess = ₹97,500
  • Result: The New Tax Regime saves ₹49,140 in cash tax because total deductions (₹4,05,000) fell below the ₹5,93,750 breakeven mark.

Three Specific Situations Where the Old Regime Still Wins

The Old Regime is not obsolete for everyone. Old Regime remains a specialized tool that wins in three specific household structures:

  1. You are servicing an active home loan on a self-occupied flat: Section 24(b) permits a deduction of up to ₹2,00,000 on home loan interest. Combine ₹2,00,000 interest with ₹1,50,000 80C principal and the ₹50,000 standard deduction, and your base deductions already hit ₹4,00,000 before adding health insurance.
  2. Paying steep metro rent in Mumbai, Bengaluru, or Gurugram: If your annual rent exceeds ₹35,000 per month, your eligible HRA exemption under Section 10(13A) frequently crosses ₹2,50,000 to ₹3,50,000. Stacked on top of 80C and 80D, total deductions easily breach ₹4,75,000. Even if you reside in a family property, our guide on HRA on rent paid to parents outlines how genuine rental arrangements legally qualify for this exemption.
  3. Paying high medical premiums for senior citizen parents: Under Section 80D, premiums paid for senior citizen parents (aged 60+) are deductible up to ₹50,000, in addition to ₹25,000 for yourself. Claiming Section 80DD or 80U for dependent disability (up to ₹1,25,000), itemized deductions exceed the New Regime advantage.

True Opportunity Cost of 80C Tax Traps

Many investors choose the Old Regime because they believe it "forces them to save." This psychological crutch comes at a devastating financial cost. To claim a ₹1.5 Lakh deduction under Section 80C, millions of Indians lock capital into traditional endowment life insurance policies or ULIPs yielding 4.5% to 5.5% annualized returns.

Doing the math reveals the damage: saving ₹46,800 in tax (at the 30% slab) by committing ₹1,50,000 annually into a 5% endowment plan produces ₹19.9 Lakh after 10 years. Switching to the New Regime, paying the ₹14,000 extra tax difference, and investing that same ₹1,50,000 into a low-cost Nifty 50 index fund (compounding historically at 12% to 13% CAGR) produces ₹29.5 Lakh over the same decade. Forcing investments to chase tax deductions costs over ₹9.6 Lakh in forfeited compound wealth.

Switching Rules: Salaried vs Business Professionals

A widespread misconception is that selecting a regime on your employer's HR payroll portal in April binds you for the year. Such an assumption is legally incorrect under Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) circulars:

For Salaried Employees (ITR-1 and ITR-2): You can select the New Regime with your employer for monthly TDS deductions, and then freely switch to the Old Regime when filing your return under Section 139(1) in July if your actual deductions turn out higher. You can switch back and forth every financial year without penalty.

For Freelancers and Business Owners (ITR-3 and ITR-4): The rule is drastically stricter under Section 115BAC(6). Non-salaried professionals with business or professional income who opt out of the New Regime to the Old Regime are granted only one lifetime opportunity to switch back to the New Regime. Once they re-enter the New Regime, they can never return to the Old Regime again unless their business income ceases permanently.

Frequently Asked Questions

My HR deducted tax under the New Regime all year. Can I file my July ITR under the Old Regime and get a refund?

Yes. The regime chosen on your company payroll portal is solely an interim estimate for monthly TDS under Section 192. When filing your ITR-1 or ITR-2 before July 31 on the income tax portal, you can select the Old Regime, enter your 80C, 80D, and HRA proofs, and the Income Tax Department will refund the excess TDS directly to your verified bank account.

Suppose someone earns ₹7.6 Lakh annual salary. Will I pay zero tax under the New Regime?

Salaried employees receive a flat ₹75,000 Standard Deduction, reducing your taxable income from ₹7,60,000 down to ₹6,85,000. Because your net taxable income (under FY 2026-27 rules) is below ₹12,00,000 in the New Regime—or below ₹5,00,000 in the Old Regime—Section 87A provides a full rebate reducing tax liability to zero.

Can I claim my home loan interest under the New Tax Regime?

No. For a self-occupied property, home loan interest under Section 24(b) (up to ₹2,00,000) is strictly disallowed under the New Regime. When your home is rented out to a tenant, interest can still be set off against actual rental income, but losses from house property cannot be set off against your salary income.

What happens to my employer NPS contribution if I switch to the New Tax Regime?

Your employer contribution to NPS under Section 80CCD(2) remains fully tax-deductible up to 14% of your Basic + Dearness Allowance (DA) under both Old and New Tax Regimes. Corporate NPS remains one of the very few corporate tax benefits preserved in the New Regime.

Assuming they have no home loan and pay ₹15,000 monthly rent. Is Old Regime worth it at ₹12 Lakh salary?

No. At ₹12 Lakh salary, your breakeven deduction is ₹3,62,500. With ₹1.5 Lakh under 80C, ₹50,000 standard deduction, and roughly ₹1,10,000 eligible HRA, your total deductions reach ₹3,10,000. Choosing the New Regime will save you approximately ₹10,920 in annual tax while freeing you from submitting rent receipts.

If I stop investing in ELSS funds because I moved to the New Regime, where should that money go?

Redirect that capital into a direct, low-cost broad-market index fund (such as UTI Nifty 50 Direct Plan) or an active flexi-cap fund (like Parag Parikh Flexi Cap Direct Plan). You eliminate the mandatory 3-year lock-in of ELSS while enjoying full liquidity and an identical long-term equity growth trajectory.

Actionable Next Step: How to Check Your Number Today

Pull out your Form 16 or December payslip today. Sum up your actual non-negotiable deductions: EPF contribution, eligible rent paid minus 10% of basic, and home loan interest certificate from your bank. If the total is less than ₹3,75,000 (for salaries up to ₹12L) or less than ₹4,25,000 (for salaries around ₹15L), log in to your employer payroll portal and switch your declaration to the New Tax Regime. Take the monthly cash savings and start a disciplined SIP in an equity index fund instead of buying insurance policies you do not need.

These figures and thresholds reflect statutory tax provisions under the Income Tax Act for FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28); your specific tax liability depends on your exact salary structure and exemptions, which a qualified Chartered Accountant can evaluate for your scenario.
