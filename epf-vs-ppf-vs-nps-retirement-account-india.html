EPF vs PPF vs NPS: Which Retirement Account Should You Prioritise in India?

By tejascopywriter Published: July 2026 Last Updated: July 2026
← Back to Articles

Here's a situation that plays out in millions of Indian households every year. Vikram is a 32-year-old software engineer in Pune, drawing a salary of ₹80,000 a month. He has an EPF account that gets funded automatically from his payroll. His mother opened a PPF account for him years ago that he tops up occasionally. And HR at his new company has been nudging him to activate NPS under the corporate scheme. He has all three accounts — and absolutely no idea which one is doing what for him, or whether he's making the right choices.

If that sounds familiar, you're not alone. Most salaried Indians accumulate EPF, PPF, and NPS almost by accident — EPF through payroll, PPF because a parent or CA suggested it, NPS because the company offered it. But very few people actually compare these three instruments side by side. Which one gives better returns? Which one has the best tax treatment? And most importantly, if you have extra money to invest each month, which one should it go into?

Let's work through each one properly, then put them head to head so you can make a clear decision.

1. EPF: The Retirement Account You Probably Don't Think About Enough

The Employees' Provident Fund — managed by EPFO (Employees' Provident Fund Organisation) — is India's most widespread retirement savings scheme. If you're a salaried employee at a company with 20 or more employees, you're almost certainly enrolled. Both you and your employer contribute 12% of your basic salary each month. So if Vikram's basic is ₹40,000, that's ₹4,800 from him and ₹4,800 matched by his employer — a total of ₹9,600 every single month, automatically.

The interest rate for 2025-26 is 8.15% per annum, compounded annually. This rate is declared by the government each year and has historically stayed between 8% and 8.5% for the past decade. The critical thing to understand is that this is a guaranteed rate — EPF doesn't fluctuate with market conditions. Your money earns exactly what the government declares, regardless of whether markets are booming or crashing.

The tax treatment is what really makes EPF exceptional. It operates under an EEE regime — Exempt-Exempt-Exempt. Your contribution qualifies for Section 80C deduction, the interest compounds completely tax-free, and the entire maturity amount is tax-free in your hands (provided you've completed five years of continuous service). Very few investment products in India can claim all three exemptions simultaneously. The employer's contribution up to ₹7,500 per month is also not taxed in your hands — an additional benefit that most employees never notice.

📊 Did You Know? As of 2026, EPFO has over 7 crore active EPF subscribers across India, making it one of the largest provident fund organisations in the world. Yet a significant number of members don't know their UAN number or the exact corpus sitting in their account right now.

The withdrawal rules deserve special attention. You cannot withdraw your full EPF before retirement (age 58) without triggering penalties and tax consequences. EPFO does allow partial withdrawals for specific life events — a medical emergency, a wedding, home purchase, or children's education. The most dangerous trap people fall into? Withdrawing their entire EPF every time they switch jobs. We'll come back to why that's such a costly mistake.

⚠️ Watch Out: Early EPF Withdrawal Is a Tax Bomb Withdrawing your EPF before completing 5 years of continuous service makes the entire amount — contributions, employer contributions, and all accumulated interest — fully taxable in the year of withdrawal. For someone in the 30% tax bracket, that can erase years of compounding in a single bad decision.

2. PPF: The Quiet Workhorse That Never Gets Enough Credit

The Public Provident Fund is a government savings scheme that anyone — salaried, self-employed, freelancer, or housewife — can open at a post office or any major bank like SBI, HDFC, or Axis Bank. Unlike EPF, it's completely voluntary. You decide how much to contribute each year, anywhere from ₹500 to a maximum of ₹1.5 lakh. The current interest rate is 7.1% per annum, reviewed by the government quarterly.

The defining feature of PPF is its 15-year lock-in. This sounds restrictive, but it's actually a feature — it forces you to stay invested and let compounding work undisturbed, without the temptation to dip in during a market scare or an impulsive purchase. After 15 years, you can extend the account in 5-year blocks, indefinitely. Partial withdrawals are permitted from Year 7 onwards, and loans against the PPF balance are available between Years 3 and 6.

Like EPF, PPF follows the EEE model. Contributions are deductible under Section 80C (up to ₹1.5L combined with other 80C investments), the interest earned is fully tax-exempt, and the maturity corpus is completely tax-free. For self-employed professionals — doctors, consultants, shop owners — who have no access to EPF, PPF is the closest equivalent and often the backbone of a well-structured retirement plan.

✓ Pro Tip: Deposit Before April 5th Every Year PPF interest is calculated on the lowest balance between the 5th and the last day of each month. If you deposit your annual amount before April 5th, your full contribution earns interest for all 12 months of that financial year. Depositing even a week late in April means you lose one full month of interest — on ₹1.5 lakh, that's about ₹888 gone, every single year.

3. NPS: The Market-Linked Retirement Account Most People Misunderstand

The National Pension System, regulated by PFRDA (Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority), is India's most sophisticated retirement savings instrument — and also the most misunderstood. Unlike EPF and PPF which offer fixed, guaranteed returns, NPS invests your money in the market through professional fund managers. Depending on your age and chosen allocation, your money is distributed across equity (up to 75% if you're under 50), government bonds, and corporate bonds.

NPS has two tiers. Tier 1 is the primary retirement account — contributions are locked until age 60, with very limited exceptions for partial withdrawal. Tier 2 is an optional savings account with no lock-in, but without the tax deduction benefits of Tier 1. For retirement planning purposes, Tier 1 is what matters. Historical returns for equity-heavy NPS portfolios have averaged around 10-12% per annum over the past decade — significantly outpacing both EPF and PPF over long time horizons.

Here's where NPS gets genuinely interesting from a tax standpoint. Your NPS Tier 1 contribution falls under Section 80CCD(1), which is part of the ₹1.5L Section 80C ceiling. But there's a second provision — Section 80CCD(1B) — which allows you to claim an additional ₹50,000 deduction on NPS contributions, completely over and above the existing ₹1.5 lakh limit. This is the only investment vehicle in India that comes with this bonus deduction. No ELSS, no PPF, no insurance plan offers it.

💡 Key Insight: The Only ₹2 Lakh Tax Deduction in India By maxing out Section 80C (₹1.5L) through EPF and PPF, and additionally putting ₹50,000 into NPS under Section 80CCD(1B), you can claim a total deduction of ₹2 lakh per year. For someone in the 30% bracket including cess, that's approximately ₹62,400 in annual tax savings — money that would otherwise go straight to the Income Tax department.

The trade-off with NPS is at maturity. When you turn 60, you can withdraw up to 60% of the corpus as a lump sum — now fully tax-free. But you're mandatorily required to use the remaining 40% to purchase an annuity from a PFRDA-empanelled insurance company. That annuity income is taxable every year as per your income slab. This is why most financial planners suggest NPS as a powerful supplement to EPF and PPF, not a complete replacement.

5. Vikram's Numbers: What Does This Look Like at Age 60?

Let's come back to Vikram in Pune. He's 32, his basic salary is ₹40,000 (part of his ₹80,000 total CTC), and he has 28 years until he retires at 60. His mandatory EPF contribution is ₹4,800 from his side and ₹4,800 matched by his employer — so ₹9,600 per month flows into EPF automatically.

If Vikram does nothing beyond the mandatory EPF and earns 8.15% consistently over 28 years, his EPF corpus at 60 works out to approximately ₹1.5 crore. That sounds decent, until you factor in 28 years of inflation. Now, separately, if Vikram also contributes ₹1.5 lakh per year to PPF (₹12,500 per month) for the same 28 years at 7.1%, he accumulates another ₹85–90 lakh. EPF plus PPF gives him roughly ₹2.3–2.4 crore across both accounts.

Here's where adding NPS changes the picture. Vikram additionally puts ₹50,000 per year into NPS Tier 1 — just enough to claim the full 80CCD(1B) deduction. Invested in an equity-heavy NPS portfolio earning 11% annually over 28 years, that ₹50,000 per year grows to approximately ₹85 lakh. His total retirement corpus across all three accounts: ₹3.1–3.2 crore. The tax saving from the extra NPS deduction — ₹15,000 per year at the 30% bracket — if reinvested in a simple equity SIP earning 12%, adds another ₹35–40 lakh. All from one smart tax decision made early in his career.

6. Which One Should You Actually Prioritise?

Here's the practical framework — not the textbook answer, but what actually makes sense for most salaried Indians building retirement wealth in 2026.

Step 1 — Never skip or reduce your EPF contribution. The mandatory 12% from you, matched by 12% from your employer, is essentially free money that goes into a tax-free, government-guaranteed account. If your employer offers VPF (Voluntary Provident Fund) — where you can contribute above the mandatory 12% at the same 8.15% rate — that often deserves serious consideration before you look elsewhere.

Step 2 — If you're in the 20% or 30% tax bracket, prioritise the NPS ₹50K before topping up PPF beyond your 80C requirement. The 80CCD(1B) deduction is a true bonus — there is no other instrument in the Indian tax code that offers it. The decades of market-linked returns (10–12% historically) inside NPS equity funds will compound into a materially larger corpus than PPF's 7.1%.

Step 3 — Use PPF as your zero-risk, ultra-stable allocation. If you're self-employed, PPF should be the bedrock of your retirement savings. If you're salaried, use whatever Section 80C headroom remains after your EPF contribution to top up PPF. The 15-year lock-in removes the temptation to raid your retirement savings during a market downturn or emotional spending moment.

✓ Pro Tip: Activate Your UAN on epfindia.gov.in Today Your Universal Account Number is your EPF identity number that follows you across every employer. Log into the EPFO member portal, link your UAN to Aadhaar, update your nominee details, and set up e-nomination. This one-time task of about 15 minutes prevents your EPF from getting stuck between job changes — a surprisingly common problem that delays people's retirement savings by months.

7. The Mistakes That Cost People Lakhs Over a Lifetime

The single biggest EPF mistake Indian employees make is withdrawing the entire balance every time they switch jobs. Someone gets a new offer, resigns, and during the gap between jobs withdraws their ₹3–4 lakh EPF balance. That ₹4 lakh — if left untouched and compounded at 8.15% for 25 more years — would have grown to ₹29–30 lakh. Instead, it's gone in a few weeks. Always transfer your EPF to the new employer's account using the EPFO online transfer facility — never withdraw unless it's a genuine emergency.

With NPS, the most common mistake is simply never activating the account. Many employees' companies offer corporate NPS with an employer contribution — essentially extra retirement savings that the company funds — and employees never complete the enrollment. If your company offers corporate NPS, register immediately. Even without employer contribution, the additional ₹50,000 deduction under 80CCD(1B) should be sufficient motivation.

For PPF, the mistake tends to be neglect. People open accounts with ₹500, forget about them, and assume they're "investing in PPF." A PPF account receiving token contributions doesn't move the needle on retirement planning. If you're going to use PPF — and you should — commit to maximising it. The full ₹1.5 lakh per year invested consistently for 20-25 years creates a corpus that would genuinely matter at retirement.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I have all three — EPF, PPF, and NPS — at the same time?

Absolutely. Having all three working in parallel is actually the recommended structure for most salaried Indians. EPF is mandatory through your employer, PPF you can open at any bank or post office, and NPS can be opened online through your bank's net banking portal or the eNPS website (nps.nsdl.com). They serve different purposes and complement each other well.

What happens to my EPF if I switch jobs?

Your EPF balance stays in your EPFO account, tied to your UAN. When you join a new employer, provide your UAN and they'll continue contributions into the same account. The most common mistake is withdrawing during the job gap — resist that. You can transfer your EPF online through the EPFO member portal. If the transfer doesn't happen automatically, you can raise a transfer claim yourself — it typically takes 20–30 working days.

Is NPS only for government employees?

NPS was originally designed for central government employees when it launched in 2004, but it was extended to the general public in 2009. Any Indian citizen between 18 and 70 years of age can now open an NPS account — private sector employees, self-employed professionals, freelancers, business owners. The extra ₹50,000 deduction under Section 80CCD(1B) is available to everyone equally.

Which gives better returns — PPF or NPS equity?

Over long time horizons of 20 years or more, NPS equity portfolios have historically delivered 10–12% returns versus PPF's 7.1% — and that difference compounds dramatically. ₹1 lakh invested at 7.1% for 25 years becomes roughly ₹5.5 lakh. The same amount at 11% becomes ₹13.6 lakh. However, NPS carries market risk and has the mandatory annuity requirement. Most investors benefit from having both rather than choosing one exclusively.

Can I claim both Section 80C and Section 80CCD(1B) deductions in the same financial year?

Yes — and this is one of the most underutilised tax benefits available to Indian taxpayers. You can claim up to ₹1.5 lakh under Section 80C (covering EPF employee contribution, PPF deposits, ELSS, life insurance premiums, and others) AND an additional ₹50,000 under Section 80CCD(1B) for NPS Tier 1 contributions. Total deduction: ₹2 lakh. For someone in the 30% bracket including cess, that's approximately ₹62,400 in annual tax savings — every year.
