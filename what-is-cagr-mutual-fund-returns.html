What is CAGR? How to Use It to Compare Mutual Fund Returns

You're comparing two mutual funds. Fund A returned 45% last year. Fund B returned 12% every single year for the past 5 years. Which one actually made you more money? The answer surprises most people — and understanding CAGR is the only way to get it right.

July 30, 2026 By tejascopywriter
← Back to Articles

Most investors look at absolute returns — the raw percentage gain over a period — to judge whether a fund performed well. It feels intuitive. 45% is bigger than 12%, so Fund A must be better, right? Not necessarily. Absolute returns strip out the most important variable in investing: time. CAGR puts time back into the equation.

Fund A with 45% in one year is fine. But Fund B with 12% every year for 5 years? That turns ₹1 lakh into ₹1,76,234. Fund A, if it delivered 0% for the other 4 years, only ends at ₹1,45,000. Fund B wins — by a large margin. This is why CAGR is the only honest metric for comparing investments across time.

What Does CAGR Stand For?

CAGR stands for Compound Annual Growth Rate. It is the single, smoothed annual rate of return that would take your investment from its starting value to its ending value, assuming it grew at the same pace every year. Think of it as the "steady speed" equivalent of a drive where you sometimes hit 120 km/h and sometimes crawl at 40 km/h.

Markets are volatile. A fund might return 35% one year, -18% the next, and 22% the year after. CAGR distills all that chaos into one representative annual figure. It does not tell you how bumpy the ride was — for that, you look at standard deviation or Sharpe ratio — but it tells you exactly where you ended up, on a per-year basis.

💡 Key Insight: SEBI and AMFI require mutual funds to disclose returns using CAGR (not absolute returns) for periods exceeding one year. This regulatory mandate exists precisely because absolute returns are misleading for multi-year comparisons.

The CAGR Formula

The formula is simpler than it looks:

CAGR = (End Value ÷ Start Value)(1/Years) − 1

Example: ₹1,00,000 grew to ₹1,76,234 in 5 years
CAGR = (1,76,234 ÷ 1,00,000)(1/5) − 1
CAGR = (1.76234)0.2 − 1
CAGR = 1.12 − 1 = 0.12 = 12% per year

You do not need to do this math manually. But understanding the formula helps you grasp what CAGR actually represents: it is the constant annual growth rate that produces the same end result as the actual, lumpy, real-world returns your fund delivered.

Why CAGR Matters More Than Absolute Returns

Here is a scenario that plays out in Indian investing communities every bull market cycle. A small-cap fund posts 80% returns in year one. Social media explodes with excitement. Thousands of investors pile in. Then the fund falls 40% in year two. "It's still up overall," many say. But is it?

₹1,00,000 at +80% becomes ₹1,80,000. Then at -40%, it falls to ₹1,08,000. The net 2-year return is only 8% absolute — or roughly 3.9% CAGR annually. A boring debt fund at 7% CAGR would have nearly matched that over the same period, with none of the stomach-churning volatility.

⚠️ Watch Out: Fund houses sometimes advertise peak-to-trough "point returns" that look spectacular. Always calculate or check the 3Y and 5Y CAGR before investing. A single-year spike followed by a crash looks terrible when you annualize it.

How Mutual Funds Use CAGR in India

AMFI (Association of Mutual Funds in India) mandates that all fund advertisements and factsheets disclose returns as CAGR for periods longer than one year. For periods of one year or less, absolute returns are shown — which is why a fund might advertise "45% returns" but then show "12% CAGR (5Y)" right next to it.

The 5-year CAGR is generally considered the most reliable metric for evaluating a fund manager's skill, because it captures at least one full market cycle including both a bull phase and a correction. A fund that delivers strong 5Y CAGR consistently — across multiple 5-year windows — is genuinely good. A fund with one spectacular year is just lucky.

CAGR vs XIRR — What's the Difference?

This is the question that confuses even experienced investors. Here is the clean distinction: CAGR works for lumpsum investments. You put in one amount on one date, you get back a final value on another date, and CAGR tells you the annual growth rate.

But SIPs are different. You invest ₹5,000 in January, another ₹5,000 in February, another in March — each installment has a different holding period. The January investment earns returns for 12 months; the December investment earns returns for only 1 month. CAGR cannot handle this.

XIRR = Extended Internal Rate of Return

XIRR accounts for the exact date and amount of every individual cash flow — every SIP installment — and calculates the annualized return that makes the net present value of all those flows equal to zero. It is the only accurate measure for SIP returns.

When you log in to Groww, Zerodha Coin, or CAMS, the return shown for your SIP portfolio is XIRR — not CAGR. If you see "Your SIP has delivered 14.3% XIRR," that is your true annualized return on your SIP investments accounting for every instalment date. Compare it to the fund's benchmark XIRR (or 5Y CAGR as a rough proxy) to see if the fund is actually adding value.

What's a "Good" CAGR for Indian Mutual Funds?

Here are realistic CAGR benchmarks for different fund categories in India, based on long-run AMFI and NSE data:

  • Nifty 50 TRI (Index Benchmark): ~12–13% CAGR over rolling 15–20 year periods
  • Large-Cap Active Funds: 12–14% CAGR (top quartile over 5Y)
  • Flexi-Cap / Multi-Cap Funds: 13–15% CAGR (varies widely by manager)
  • Small-Cap Funds: 15–18% CAGR in bull markets (but far higher volatility and drawdowns)
  • Balanced Advantage Funds: 9–11% CAGR (smoother ride, lower peaks)
  • Bank Fixed Deposits: 6.5–7.5% (guaranteed, but inflation-adjusted returns are near zero)
✓ Pro Tip: A fund that consistently beats its benchmark (say, the Nifty 50) by 1.5–2% per year over a 10-year period is genuinely exceptional. Most active funds fail this test after accounting for expense ratios. This is why many savvy Indian investors prefer low-cost index funds that simply match the benchmark CAGR.

How to Use CAGR to Compare Two Funds

Here is a practical, step-by-step method to compare two mutual funds using CAGR:

  1. Pull the fund factsheets: Go to AMFI's website (amfiindia.com) or the AMC's own website. Download the latest factsheet for both funds you are comparing.
  2. Find the 5-year CAGR: Every factsheet shows 1Y, 3Y, and 5Y CAGR returns. Focus on the 5Y CAGR — it represents one full market cycle and filters out lucky one-year spikes.
  3. Compare both to the Nifty 50 benchmark: The factsheet also shows the benchmark's return over the same period. If Fund A delivered 13.5% but the Nifty 50 delivered 12.8%, Fund A added 0.7% alpha — which is barely meaningful after accounting for higher expense ratio.
  4. Check the expense ratio: A fund with 14% CAGR and a 1.8% expense ratio is delivering 12.2% net returns. An index fund with 12.5% CAGR and a 0.1% expense ratio delivers 12.4% net. The "worse" CAGR fund is actually better.
  5. Look at rolling returns, not just point-to-point: A fund that delivered 14% CAGR from January 2019 to January 2024 might show 8% CAGR from July 2019 to July 2024. Rolling 5Y returns (checked across different start dates) give a far more honest picture of consistency.
💡 Key Insight: When two funds have similar 5Y CAGRs, pick the one with the lower expense ratio. In long-term compounding, a 0.5% lower expense ratio compounds into lakhs of rupees of additional wealth over 20+ years. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between CAGR and absolute return?

Absolute return shows total percentage gain over the full holding period with no reference to time. CAGR annualizes that gain to a per-year equivalent, making comparisons across different holding periods meaningful. A 100% absolute return over 10 years is only 7.2% CAGR — far less impressive than it initially sounds.

Why is XIRR used for SIPs instead of CAGR?

CAGR assumes a single lumpsum invested at one point in time. SIPs involve multiple investments at different dates, each with a different holding period. XIRR (Extended Internal Rate of Return) accounts for the precise timing and amount of each cash flow, making it the only accurate measure for SIP portfolio returns.

Can CAGR be negative?

Yes. If your investment's end value is lower than its start value, CAGR is negative. If ₹1,00,000 fell to ₹80,000 over 5 years, the CAGR is approximately -4.4% per year. Negative CAGR simply means the fund lost value on an annualized basis over that period.

What is a good CAGR for a mutual fund in India?

A good equity fund CAGR benchmark is consistent outperformance versus the Nifty 50 TRI (~12–13% long-run CAGR). Large-cap active funds delivering 13–14% CAGR over 5–10 years are considered strong performers. But always compare net-of-expense-ratio returns, not gross CAGR.

How do I find the CAGR of a mutual fund?

Every fund's factsheet — available on AMFI's website (amfiindia.com) or the AMC's site — shows 1Y, 3Y, and 5Y CAGR figures. You can also find these on Groww, Zerodha Coin, or MF Central. Always compare the fund's CAGR to its benchmark index CAGR over the same period to assess true alpha.
