CTC vs In-Hand Salary: Why Your Offer Letter Number Is Never What Hits Your Bank Account

Cost to Company (CTC) represents the total annual expenditure an employer incurs on an employee, including non-cash retiral provisions like employer EPF (12% of basic) and statutory gratuity (15/26 of monthly basic), alongside health insurance and variable performance bonuses. Your actual in-hand take-home pay is significantly lower after subtracting these employer retiral costs, mandatory employee EPF contributions, state professional tax, and income tax TDS.

A ₹12 Lakh CTC offer sounds like ₹1 Lakh a month. In practice, most employees see somewhere between ₹75,000 and ₹85,000 land in their account. Such a gap does not stem from a bank error or hidden fee; rather, it reflects the deliberate architecture of Indian salary structures. Here is where every rupee actually flows.

Key Takeaways: What You Need to Know First

  • 1. CTC ≠ what you're paid: CTC includes employer costs you never touch monthly, like employer EPF contribution, gratuity provisioning, and often insurance premiums.
  • 2. The typical drop is 18-25%: For most salaried employees in the ₹8-25 LPA range, in-hand annual salary runs 18-25% below the headline CTC figure.
  • 3. Gratuity is a 5-year lock: Roughly 4.81% of your Basic Salary is provisioned as gratuity inside your CTC, but you legally receive it only after 5 years of continuous service.
  • 4. Variable pay is rarely 100% Fixed: A CTC showing full "Target Bonus" doesn't mean full payout , actual payouts commonly run 70-90% of target depending on company and individual performance.
  • 5. Two identical CTCs can differ in hand: The Basic Salary percentage, employer PF/NPS structuring, and tax regime declared all change your actual monthly credit even at the same CTC.

Why the "CTC Shock" Happens to Almost Everyone

Every year, freshers and job-switchers sign offer letters quoting an annual CTC, divide by 12 in their heads, and expect that exact figure on payday. Far from an employer trying to shortchange you, this mismatch simply reflects how "Cost to Company" is legally and structurally designed. CTC is not a salary figure; it's the total annual cost the employer incurs to employ you, which includes money paid directly to you, money set aside on your behalf for retirement and gratuity, and money spent on your benefits like insurance. Only a portion of that total ever converts into a monthly bank credit.

What Actually Makes Up Your CTC

Most Indian salary structures break CTC into these broad buckets:

  • Basic Salary: Usually 40-50% of CTC. It forms the base figure for everything else. HRA, EPF, and gratuity are all calculated as a percentage of Basic.
  • House Rent Allowance (HRA): Typically 40% or 50% of Basic depending on whether you're in a non-metro or metro city.
  • Special Allowance / Flexible Pay: The balancing figure that makes the components add up to the total CTC. Fully paid out monthly and fully taxable.
  • Employer's EPF Contribution: 12% of Basic Salary (or ₹1,800/month for lower Basic slabs under the wage ceiling), paid by the company into your provident fund account. Part of CTC, never touches your bank account monthly.
  • Gratuity Provisioning: Approximately 4.81% of Basic Salary, set aside notionally for a future gratuity payout. Part of CTC on paper only.
  • Variable Pay / Performance Bonus: Often shown at 100% of target inside CTC, but paid based on actual performance , company-wide and individual.
  • Other Perquisites: Employer NPS contribution, meal cards, LTA, group insurance premiums, which are all technically part of CTC, rarely part of your monthly credit.

Gross Salary: The First Deduction You Never See

Before you even get to your payslip's "deductions" section, your CTC has already shrunk. Gross Salary = CTC − Employer's EPF Contribution − Gratuity Provisioning − Other Employer-Only Contributions (like employer NPS or insurance premiums the company pays directly to the insurer). That gross figure is what actually shows up at the top of your monthly payslip as "Total Earnings" , and it is already meaningfully lower than your annual CTC divided by 12.

From this gross figure, your payslip then subtracts your own deductions: your employee EPF contribution (typically another 12% of Basic), Professional Tax (a small state-levied tax, commonly ₹200/month in states that impose it, nil in others), and TDS on salary based on your projected annual tax liability. What remains after all of this is your net in-hand salary , the actual credit to your bank account.

The Gratuity Trap: Money in Your CTC You May Never Get

Gratuity provisioning is the single most misunderstood line item in an Indian CTC breakup. Companies are required to add roughly 4.81% of your Basic Salary into your CTC to represent the cost of funding your eventual gratuity liability under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972. This looks like "your money" on the offer letter. You cannot access this money in any practical sense: employees become legally entitled to receive it only after completing 5 years of continuous service with that employer and then resigning, retiring, or getting terminated (other than for misconduct). Leave before 5 years , which the large majority of employees under 30 do at least once , and this entire CTC component simply evaporates; you never see a rupee of it and it isn't refunded or reallocated to you in any form.

The Variable Pay Illusion

Offer letters frequently state a CTC that already assumes 100% payout of your Target Variable Pay or Performance Bonus. In reality, variable pay is tied to a mix of company performance, business unit performance, and individual rating , and actual payouts commonly range from 70% to 90% of target in a normal year, sometimes lower in a weak year for the company. A CTC of ₹15 Lakh with ₹2 Lakh variable pay component, therefore, might realistically deliver only ₹1.4-1.7 Lakh of that ₹2 Lakh in an average year , a gap that never shows up until the payout cycle actually happens. When comparing two job offers, always ask HR for the Fixed fixed component separately from the CTC headline number.

Full Worked Example: ₹12 LPA CTC to In-Hand

Consider a typical structure for an employee with a ₹12,00,000 annual CTC in a metro city, filing under the Old Tax Regime with standard deduction and a modest 80C claim:

  • Annual CTC: ₹12,00,000
  • Less: Employer EPF contribution (12% of ₹4,80,000 Basic): −₹57,600
  • Less: Gratuity provisioning (4.81% of Basic): −₹23,088
  • = Gross Annual Salary: ₹11,19,312 (this is what appears as "Total Earnings" across 12 payslips)
  • Less: Employee EPF contribution (12% of Basic): −₹57,600
  • Less: Professional Tax (approx., state-dependent): −₹2,400
  • Less: Estimated TDS on salary (Old Regime, after standard deduction and 80C): approx. −₹75,000
  • = Approximate Net Annual In-Hand: ₹9,84,312, or roughly ₹82,000 per month

That is a gap of nearly ₹2.16 Lakh, roughly 18%, between the CTC figure quoted at hiring and the money that actually reaches the bank account over the year. The exact numbers shift with your Basic Salary percentage, city, tax regime choice, and any voluntary PF or NPS contributions, but the direction and rough magnitude of the gap holds across most mid-level salary brackets in India.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my in-hand salary so much lower than my CTC?

CTC includes several amounts you never receive as monthly cash: the employer's EPF contribution, gratuity provisioning, and often insurance premiums and NPS contributions the company makes on your behalf. Your in-hand salary is what remains after subtracting all these employer-side components plus your own EPF contribution, professional tax, and TDS.

Does a higher CTC always mean a higher in-hand salary?

Two offers with the same CTC can produce very different in-hand amounts depending on how much of the CTC is Basic Salary (which drives EPF and gratuity deductions) versus fixed allowances, and how much is Fixed fixed pay versus variable bonus that isn't paid monthly.

Is the employer's EPF contribution taxable to me?

Generally no, up to a combined limit. Under Section 17(2)(vii), if your employer's total contribution to EPF, NPS, and superannuation fund together exceeds ₹7,50,000 in a financial year, the excess amount is taxable as a perquisite in your hands, along with the interest earned on it.

What is the gratuity component shown in my CTC?

Gratuity represents a notional provisioning of roughly 4.81% of your Basic Salary that employers set aside to fund future liabilities under the Payment of Gratuity Act. While listed in your CTC as an employment expense, it never reaches your monthly paycheck. You receive it only upon leaving or retiring after at least 5 years of continuous service.

Why does my in-hand salary change even when my CTC stays the same?

The most common reason is a change in TDS as the financial year progresses, or a change in the tax regime you declared to your employer. Variable pay payout percentage, revised professional tax slabs, or a mid-year change in voluntary PF contribution can also alter the monthly in-hand figure even when total CTC is unchanged.

Actionable Next Step: Ask for the Full Break-Up Before You Sign

Before accepting your next offer, request a detailed CTC break-up, not just the headline number. Specifically ask for: the Basic Salary percentage, the Fixed fixed pay excluding variable/bonus, and whether gratuity and employer PF are included inside or outside the quoted CTC figure. Two offers that look identical on the top line can differ by ₹1-2 Lakh in actual annual take-home once you see the real structure underneath.

These figures are illustrative and based on common Indian salary structuring practices for FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28). Actual Basic Salary percentages, professional tax rates, and TDS vary by employer, state, and individual tax declarations , verify your specific breakup with your employer's payroll team.
