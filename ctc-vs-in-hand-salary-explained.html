CTC vs In-Hand Salary: Why Your Offer Letter Number Is Never What Hits Your Bank Account

Hi, I'm Tejas — an engineering student researching personal finance. This page explains how a financial concept works based on publicly available regulations and mathematical models. It is written with AI assistance and checked against official sources. It is purely educational and does not constitute investment, tax, or legal advice. A ₹12 Lakh CTC offer sounds like ₹1 Lakh a month. In practice, most employees see somewhere between ₹75,000 and ₹85,000 land in their account. The gap isn't a bank error or hidden fee — it's built into the structure of every Indian salary slip. Here is exactly where that money goes, component by component.

Key Takeaways: What You Need to Know First

  • 1. CTC ≠ what you're paid: CTC includes employer costs you never touch monthly — employer EPF contribution, gratuity provisioning, and often insurance premiums.
  • 2. The typical drop is 18-25%: For most salaried employees in the ₹8-25 LPA range, in-hand annual salary runs 18-25% below the headline CTC figure.
  • 3. Gratuity is a 5-year lock: Roughly 4.81% of your Basic Salary is provisioned as gratuity inside your CTC, but you legally receive it only after 5 years of continuous service.
  • 4. Variable pay is rarely 100% Fixed: A CTC showing full "Target Bonus" doesn't mean full payout — actual payouts commonly run 70-90% of target depending on company and individual performance.
  • 5. Two identical CTCs can differ in hand: The Basic Salary percentage, employer PF/NPS structuring, and tax regime declared all change your actual monthly credit even at the same CTC.

Why the "CTC Shock" Happens to Almost Everyone

Every year, freshers and job-switchers sign offer letters quoting an annual CTC, do the mental division by 12, and expect that exact monthly number in their bank account on payday. It never arrives. This isn't a company shortchanging anyone — it's how "Cost to Company" is legally and structurally defined. CTC is not a salary figure; it's the total annual cost the employer incurs to employ you, which includes money paid directly to you, money set aside on your behalf for retirement and gratuity, and money spent on your benefits like insurance. Only a portion of that total ever converts into a monthly bank credit.

What Actually Makes Up Your CTC

Most Indian salary structures — with some variation by company — break CTC into these broad buckets:

  • Basic Salary: Usually 40-50% of CTC. This is the anchor figure — HRA, EPF, and gratuity are all calculated as a percentage of Basic.
  • House Rent Allowance (HRA): Typically 40% or 50% of Basic depending on whether you're in a non-metro or metro city.
  • Special Allowance / Flexible Pay: The balancing figure that makes the components add up to the total CTC. Fully paid out monthly and fully taxable.
  • Employer's EPF Contribution: 12% of Basic Salary (or ₹1,800/month for lower Basic slabs under the wage ceiling), paid by the company into your provident fund account. Part of CTC, never touches your bank account monthly.
  • Gratuity Provisioning: Approximately 4.81% of Basic Salary, set aside notionally for a future gratuity payout. Part of CTC on paper only.
  • Variable Pay / Performance Bonus: Often shown at 100% of target inside CTC, but paid based on actual performance — company-wide and individual.
  • Other Perquisites: Employer NPS contribution, meal cards, LTA, group insurance premiums — all technically part of CTC, rarely part of your monthly credit.

Gross Salary: The First Deduction You Never See

Before you even get to your payslip's "deductions" section, your CTC has already shrunk. Gross Salary = CTC − Employer's EPF Contribution − Gratuity Provisioning − Other Employer-Only Contributions (like employer NPS or insurance premiums the company pays directly to the insurer). This gross figure is what actually shows up at the top of your monthly payslip as "Total Earnings" — and it is already meaningfully lower than your annual CTC divided by 12.

From this gross figure, your payslip then subtracts your own deductions: your employee EPF contribution (typically another 12% of Basic), Professional Tax (a small state-levied tax, commonly ₹200/month in states that impose it, nil in others), and TDS on salary based on your projected annual tax liability. What remains after all of this is your net in-hand salary — the actual credit to your bank account.

The Gratuity Trap: Money in Your CTC You May Never Get

Gratuity provisioning is the single most misunderstood line item in an Indian CTC breakup. Companies are required to add roughly 4.81% of your Basic Salary into your CTC to represent the cost of funding your eventual gratuity liability under the Payment of Gratuity Act, 1972. This looks like "your money" on the offer letter. It is not accessible money in any practical sense: you are legally entitled to receive it only if you complete 5 years of continuous service with that employer and then resign, retire, or are terminated (other than for misconduct). Leave before 5 years — which the large majority of employees under 30 do at least once — and this entire CTC component simply evaporates; you never see a rupee of it and it isn't refunded or reallocated to you in any form.

The Variable Pay Illusion

Offer letters frequently state a CTC that already assumes 100% payout of your Target Variable Pay or Performance Bonus. In reality, variable pay is tied to a mix of company performance, business unit performance, and individual rating — and actual payouts commonly range from 70% to 90% of target in a normal year, sometimes lower in a weak year for the company. A CTC of ₹15 Lakh with ₹2 Lakh variable pay component, therefore, might realistically deliver only ₹1.4-1.7 Lakh of that ₹2 Lakh in an average year — a gap that never shows up until the payout cycle actually happens. When comparing two job offers, always ask HR for the Fixed fixed component separately from the CTC headline number.

Full Worked Example: ₹12 LPA CTC to In-Hand

Consider a typical structure for an employee with a ₹12,00,000 annual CTC in a metro city, filing under the Old Tax Regime with standard deduction and a modest 80C claim:

  • Annual CTC: ₹12,00,000
  • Less: Employer EPF contribution (12% of ₹4,80,000 Basic): −₹57,600
  • Less: Gratuity provisioning (4.81% of Basic): −₹23,088
  • = Gross Annual Salary: ₹11,19,312 (this is what appears as "Total Earnings" across 12 payslips)
  • Less: Employee EPF contribution (12% of Basic): −₹57,600
  • Less: Professional Tax (approx., state-dependent): −₹2,400
  • Less: Estimated TDS on salary (Old Regime, after standard deduction and 80C): approx. −₹75,000
  • = Approximate Net Annual In-Hand: ₹9,84,312, or roughly ₹82,000 per month

That is a gap of nearly ₹2.16 Lakh — or 18% — between the CTC figure quoted at hiring and the money that actually reaches the bank account over the year. The exact numbers shift with your Basic Salary percentage, city, tax regime choice, and any voluntary PF or NPS contributions, but the direction and rough magnitude of the gap holds across most mid-level salary brackets in India.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my in-hand salary so much lower than my CTC?

CTC includes several amounts you never receive as monthly cash: the employer's EPF contribution, gratuity provisioning, and often insurance premiums and NPS contributions the company makes on your behalf. Your in-hand salary is what remains after subtracting all these employer-side components plus your own EPF contribution, professional tax, and TDS.

Does a higher CTC always mean a higher in-hand salary?

Not necessarily. Two offers with the same CTC can produce very different in-hand amounts depending on how much of the CTC is Basic Salary (which drives EPF and gratuity deductions) versus fixed allowances, and how much is Fixed fixed pay versus variable bonus that isn't paid monthly.

Is the employer's EPF contribution taxable to me?

Generally no, up to a combined limit. Under Section 17(2)(vii), if your employer's total contribution to EPF, NPS, and superannuation fund together exceeds ₹7,50,000 in a financial year, the excess amount is taxable as a perquisite in your hands, along with the interest earned on it.

What is the gratuity component shown in my CTC?

It is a notional provisioning of roughly 4.81% of your Basic Salary that your employer sets aside to fund a future gratuity payout under the Payment of Gratuity Act. It is included in your CTC as a cost to the company but is not paid to you monthly — you receive it only on resignation, retirement, or termination after completing 5 years of continuous service.

How to Think About This Yourself

When reviewing an offer letter, evaluate the Fixed Monthly Gross rather than the annual CTC. Check the Basic Salary percentage (which anchors PF and gratuity), assess whether performance bonuses are guaranteed or variable, and run an income tax calculation under both the Old and New tax regimes to forecast your exact bank credit.
