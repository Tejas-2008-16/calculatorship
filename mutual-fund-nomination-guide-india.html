Mutual Fund Nomination vs Legal Heir: Why Most Indians Have It Wrong

Millions of Indians believe that adding a nominee to their mutual fund folio settles the question of who gets their money after they die. It does not. A nominee is a trustee who temporarily holds the assets — the legal heirs are still the ultimate owners, and if they are different from the nominee, a dispute is almost certain. This guide explains exactly what nomination does and doesn't do, how to set it up correctly, and what you actually need to make your mutual fund wealth transfer smooth and dispute-free.

Key Takeaways: What You Need to Know First

  • Nominee ≠ owner. A nominee receives the units temporarily and must hand them to the legal heirs. This was confirmed by the Supreme Court in Sarbati Devi vs Usha Devi (1984) and reinforced in subsequent SEBI and MF Tribunal orders.
  • Legal heirs are determined by your personal law or Will — not by who you named as nominee.
  • No nominee = frozen folio. Since SEBI's 2022 mandatory nomination circular, folios without a nominee or an opt-out declaration are frozen for transactions.
  • You can add up to 3 nominees with specified percentage allocation from each folio.
  • A Will is still essential even with a nominee — it prevents disputes about ultimate ownership once the nominee has received the units.

The Misunderstanding That Freezes Family Wealth

Imagine a father who has been investing ₹20,000 a month in SIPs for fifteen years. He named his son as nominee across all his folios. He assumed his son would simply receive the units upon his death and continue the family's financial security. He passed away without a Will. What actually happened: the nominee (his son) had to go through a formal transmission process with every AMC, the father's daughter from his first marriage contested the assets as a legal heir under Hindu Succession Act, and the folios were frozen for nearly eight months during litigation.

This is not a rare story. It plays out in India every single year — across mutual funds, demat accounts, and fixed deposits — because the word "nominee" has been culturally misunderstood to mean "heir." It does not. Understanding the exact legal difference between these two roles is the starting point for protecting your family's financial future.

What a Nominee Actually Means in Indian Law

The legal position on nominees in India is settled, though not widely understood. A nominee is appointed under contract law — specifically, the terms of the mutual fund's Scheme Information Document and the Regulations under SEBI (Mutual Funds) Regulations, 1996. When an investor dies, the nominee is authorised to receive the mutual fund units (or their redemption proceeds) from the AMC. This makes the operational handover efficient — the AMC does not need to wait for a court order or succession certificate for smaller folios if a nominee is registered.

But receiving the units and owning the units are two entirely different things. The law has consistently held that a nominee acts as a trustee for the legal heirs — meaning the nominee holds the assets in trust and has a legal obligation to distribute them to the actual heirs as determined by the investor's personal law (Hindu Succession Act, Indian Succession Act, Muslim Personal Law, etc.) or by the investor's Will if one exists.

This distinction was established clearly in the Supreme Court's judgment in Sarbati Devi vs Usha Devi (1984), which held that a nominee under a life insurance policy cannot override the legal heirs' entitlement. The same principle has been applied by courts and tribunals to mutual fund nominations. SEBI itself acknowledged this in a consultation paper that preceded its 2022 mandatory nomination circular.

Who Is the Legal Heir and How Is That Decided?

If you die without a Will (intestate), your legal heirs are determined by the personal law that applies to you:

For Hindus, Buddhists, Jains, and Sikhs — the Hindu Succession Act, 1956 applies. Class I legal heirs are: your son, daughter, widow/widower, mother, son of a predeceased son, daughter of a predeceased son, widow of a predeceased son, and several other specified relations. All Class I heirs inherit simultaneously and equally if they exist.

For Christians and Parsis — the Indian Succession Act, 1925 applies. The spouse and children typically share the estate in specified proportions depending on whether both parents survive.

For Muslims — Muslim Personal Law (Shariat) Application Act, 1937 governs. Inheritance shares are specified in religious law and vary depending on gender and the family composition at the time of death.

If you do have a valid, registered Will, that Will overrides the intestacy rules — and it also overrides what your nominee registration says about ultimate ownership. This is the most important point: a Will, properly executed, is the most powerful estate planning tool available to an Indian investor, and it takes precedence over every other nominee registration across every financial account you own.

What Actually Happens to Your MF Units When You Die

When an AMC receives notice of an investor's death (typically through a death certificate submission), the folio is flagged and all transactions — purchases, redemptions, switches — are halted pending resolution. From this point, there are two paths depending on whether a nominee exists.

If a nominee is registered: The nominee approaches the AMC with the death certificate, their own KYC documents, and a transmission request form. For folios below a threshold amount (typically ₹5 lakh, though this varies by AMC), most fund houses process the transmission within 15–30 working days without requiring a succession certificate. Above the threshold, additional documentation like an indemnity bond or succession certificate may be required. Once the units are transmitted to the nominee, the nominee holds them as trustee for the legal heirs and must distribute as per the Will or personal law.

If no nominee is registered: The legal heirs must approach the AMC with the death certificate, legal heir certificate or succession certificate (issued by a court or revenue authority depending on the jurisdiction and asset value), Will and probate (if a Will exists), and KYC of all claimants. This process is significantly longer and more expensive, often taking 3–12 months depending on whether heirs are in agreement or not.

SEBI's Mandatory Nomination Rule: What Changed

In March 2022, SEBI issued a circular requiring all individual mutual fund investors to either register a nominee or explicitly submit a declaration opting out of nomination for each of their folios. This was a direct response to the large number of unclaimed assets sitting in frozen folios — a chronic problem in India's financial system that runs into thousands of crores of rupees.

The rule applied to existing folios (opened before March 2022) as well as new ones. The initial deadline was August 2022, which was extended multiple times due to operational challenges. The final extended deadline for compliance was June 30, 2024. Folios that were not compliant by that deadline — meaning no nominee registered and no opt-out declaration submitted — were frozen for fresh purchases, redemptions, and SIP registrations.

If your folio is currently frozen because of this rule, the process to unfreeze is straightforward: log in to your AMC's website or the CAMS/KFintech portal, navigate to the nomination section, add a nominee with their PAN and date of birth, and complete e-sign or OTP verification. Alternatively, download the opt-out declaration form, fill it, and submit it to the AMC by physical or digital means. Unfreezing typically happens within 2–5 working days.

How to Add or Update Your Nominee Online

The process varies slightly between platforms but follows the same general flow:

Through CAMS Online (camsonline.com): Log in with your registered email or mobile. Select "Service Requests" → "Nominee Registration/Change." Select the folio and AMC. Enter nominee details: full name, date of birth, relationship with the investor, PAN (mandatory as of 2022), and allocation percentage. Complete verification via OTP sent to your registered mobile or email. Confirmation comes within 24–48 hours.

Through KFintech (kfintech.com): Similar flow — log in → Transactions → Nominee Registration. Follow the prompts and verify via OTP.

Through AMC websites directly: Each AMC (SBI MF, HDFC MF, ICICI Prudential, Mirae, etc.) has a nominee management section in their investor portal. Log in with your folio number and PAN.

Through your broker or distributor: If you invested through Zerodha, Groww, Kuvera, or another platform, they typically offer nomination management within the platform itself. Check your account settings or the service requests section.

One practical tip: if you have folios across multiple AMCs and service through both CAMS and KFintech, you will need to register nominees separately on both platforms since they maintain different folios. A single CAMS login covers all CAMS-serviced AMCs, and a single KFintech login covers all KFintech-serviced AMCs.

Multiple Nominees: How to Split and Why It Matters

SEBI regulations allow up to three nominees per folio, with the investor specifying the percentage allocation for each. This is an underused feature. Most investors register only one nominee — usually a spouse — without thinking about what happens if the nominee predeceases the investor, or about how to allocate across multiple intended beneficiaries.

Consider an investor with two children and a spouse. She could register all three as nominees with allocations of 50% to the spouse and 25% each to the two children. If she does this, and the nominees match the intended heirs, the transmission after her death is cleaner and far less likely to result in disputes — because the AMC transfers the units directly in the specified proportions, and each nominee (acting as trustee) holds their portion for themselves as the intended beneficiaries.

The limitation: if a nominee dies before the investor and the investor has not updated the nomination, that nominee's portion reverts to the investor's estate and must go through the full legal heir determination process. This is why reviewing and updating your nominees every two to three years — or after any significant life event like a marriage, divorce, birth, or death in the family — is essential.

Nomination vs Will: You Need Both

The most common error in succession planning is treating nomination and Will as alternatives. They are not. They serve different purposes and you need both.

Nomination provides operational ease. When you die, the nominee can approach the AMC, submit basic documents, and receive the units or redemption proceeds relatively quickly. Without a nominee, this process gets expensive and slow. Nomination is your family's access mechanism.

A Will establishes ultimate ownership. The Will (ideally registered) specifies who your assets ultimately belong to and in what proportion. A properly drafted Will prevents any ambiguity about whether the nominee is the owner or just the trustee — it makes the nominee's trustee role explicit and names the actual beneficiaries. This eliminates the scenario where a nominee claims ownership and the rest of the family has no clear recourse.

The ideal structure is: register nominees across all your financial accounts — mutual funds, bank accounts, demat accounts, PPF, insurance policies — and separately execute a registered Will that specifies your intended beneficiaries. The Will should ideally reference your financial accounts and state that any nominee is receiving the assets as trustee for the specified heirs. If your nominee and intended heir are the same person (e.g., your spouse is both the nominee and the sole intended heir), much of this complexity disappears — but the Will still protects against scenarios where the intended heir predeceases you.

The Transmission Process: What Heirs Must Do

When the time comes, here is what the nominee or legal heirs need to do to transmit mutual fund units:

Documents required (nominee present):

  • Death certificate of the investor (original or notarised copy)
  • Transmission request form (available on AMC website or CAMS/KFintech)
  • KYC documents of the nominee (PAN, Aadhaar, passport-size photo)
  • Bank account details of the nominee (cancelled cheque or bank statement)
  • For folios above AMC-specified threshold: indemnity bond on stamp paper, or a succession certificate/probate

Documents required (no nominee, legal heirs claiming):

  • Death certificate
  • Legal heir certificate from revenue authority, or succession certificate from civil court
  • Indemnity bond signed by all legal heirs
  • Affidavit of heirship (if the AMC requires it)
  • KYC of all claimants
  • Probate of Will (if a Will exists and the AMC requires it, especially for larger folios)

For folios in demat form (where units are held in a demat account rather than AMC folios directly), the transmission process goes through the depository participant (broker or bank) rather than the AMC, and similar documentation is required.

One frequently overlooked step: after units are transmitted to the nominee or heir, they may need to do their own KYC with the AMC before they can redeem or switch the units. This is especially true for first-time investors who had never opened a mutual fund account in their own name. Plan for this 5–10 working day KYC step when timing a redemption.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does a nominee become the owner of mutual fund units after the investor's death?

No. A nominee is a trustee or custodian, not the legal owner. The nominee receives the units or their redemption proceeds to hold on behalf of the legal heirs. If the legal heirs (determined by personal law or a Will) are different from the nominee, the nominee is legally bound to hand over the assets to the heirs. The Supreme Court of India has affirmed this position in multiple cases.

What happens to mutual fund units if there is no nominee?

If an investor dies without a nominee, the fund house will freeze the folio pending a formal transmission request. The legal heirs must approach the AMC with a succession certificate or probate of Will, death certificate, and KYC documents. This process can take months and is significantly more complex and expensive than a nominee-based transmission.

Can I add a nominee to an existing mutual fund folio online?

Yes. You can update or add nominees online through the AMC's website, CAMS Online (camsonline.com), KFintech (kfintech.com), or your broker's platform. Log in with your folio-linked email or PAN, navigate to the nomination section, add up to three nominees with their PAN and percentage allocation, and complete e-sign or OTP verification. The change typically reflects within 24–48 hours.

Is a Will more important than a mutual fund nominee?

Yes, in terms of legal ownership. A properly executed and registered Will overrides the nominee for purposes of who ultimately owns the assets. However, a Will alone does not give heirs quick access to the funds — they still need probate or a succession certificate, which takes time. The best practice is to have both: a nominee for operational speed, and a Will to clearly establish ultimate ownership.

What is the SEBI rule on mandatory nomination for mutual funds?

SEBI issued a circular in 2022 making it mandatory for all existing individual mutual fund folios to either add a nominee or submit an opt-out declaration. The final deadline was June 30, 2024. Folios that did not comply were frozen for fresh purchases and redemptions. Investors with frozen folios need to add a nominee or submit an opt-out declaration through the AMC or CAMS/KFintech to unfreeze.

The Checklist: What to Do This Week

If this article has raised even a single question mark about your own nomination status, here is the minimum you should do:

  • Log in to CAMS and KFintech and check every folio. Look for the nomination status column. Any folio marked "No Nominee" or "Frozen" needs immediate attention.
  • Verify that nominees' PAN details are correctly entered. A wrong PAN on a nominee registration can cause transmission delays.
  • If you have minor children as nominees, ensure you have also specified the name of a guardian, since a minor cannot claim assets directly — the guardian acts on their behalf until they turn 18.
  • If you do not have a Will, consult a lawyer to draft and register one. A basic Will that lists your financial accounts and specifies beneficiaries can be registered at your local sub-registrar's office for a few hundred rupees and takes less than a day. The peace of mind it provides is worth far more.
  • Review your nomination across all financial instruments — not just mutual funds, but also bank accounts, FDs, demat accounts, PPF, NPS, and life insurance policies. Each has its own nomination mechanism and each needs to be consistent with your overall estate plan.
