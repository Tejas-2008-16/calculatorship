Form 26AS vs AIS vs TIS: How to Reconcile All Three Before Filing Your ITR

Every year, crores of Indians download one document, ignore the other two, and then wonder why they received a tax notice six months after filing. Form 26AS, AIS, and TIS are not three names for the same thing — they pull from different data sources, cover different time windows, and often show different numbers for the same income. This is the complete guide to understanding what each one is, where the differences come from, and the exact order in which to reconcile them before hitting Submit on your ITR.

Key Takeaways: What You Need to Know First

  • Form 26AS is the official TDS/TCS statement under Section 203AA — your lenders, banks, and visa offices accept it as proof.
  • AIS (Annual Information Statement) is a comprehensive feed of all financial data linked to your PAN — including mutual fund transactions, dividend payouts, property purchases, and foreign remittances.
  • TIS (Taxpayer Information Summary) is a cleaned-up, aggregated version of AIS — this is what pre-fills in your ITR if you use the e-filing portal's auto-fill feature.
  • All three can show different numbers for the same income. This is normal — but every difference needs an explanation before you file.
  • If your ITR figures don't match AIS, you may receive a notice under Section 143(1)(a). Reconciling first prevents this entirely.

Why Does the Tax Department Have Three Separate Documents?

The answer is evolution. Form 26AS was designed in the early 2000s when TDS was the primary compliance mechanism — it was essentially a mirror of what your employer, bank, or buyer deducted from your income and deposited with the government. It was useful, but narrow. It told you nothing about income for which no TDS was deducted, like dividends, mutual fund redemptions, or property transactions.

As India's financial infrastructure matured and data reporting obligations expanded under the Finance Act 2020 and subsequent CBDT notifications, the Income Tax Department began receiving transaction-level data from brokers, registrars, mutual fund houses, banks, and property registrars. This data — far richer than the old TDS feed — needed a new home. That home became the Annual Information Statement (AIS), launched in November 2021. And because AIS is raw and verbose (it sometimes shows the same transaction twice from two different sources), TIS was introduced as a summarized layer on top of it.

So the hierarchy is: Form 26AS gives you the TDS picture. AIS gives you the complete financial picture. TIS gives you AIS in a format that is directly comparable to ITR schedules. You need all three — and you need them to agree before you file.

Form 26AS: The Original TDS Statement

Form 26AS is the tax passbook that the Income Tax Department has maintained for each PAN since 2003. It is governed by Section 203AA of the Income Tax Act and Rule 31AB of the Income Tax Rules. Every time a deductor — your employer, a bank paying you FD interest, a tenant paying you rent above ₹50,000 per month, a buyer purchasing your property — deducts tax at source and deposits it with the government under your PAN, that entry appears in your Form 26AS.

What Form 26AS contains today:

  • Part A: TDS deducted by all deductors (salary, bank interest, rent, professional fees, etc.)
  • Part A1: TDS on sale of immovable property (Section 194IA)
  • Part B: TCS (Tax Collected at Source) on high-value transactions like foreign remittances, luxury car purchases, and overseas education
  • Part C: Tax paid by you directly — advance tax and self-assessment tax challans
  • Part D: Refunds issued in previous years
  • Part E: High-value financial transactions (AIR data — Annual Information Return)
  • Part F: TDS on sale of immovable property by buyer
  • Part G: TDS defaults (if any deductor has not deposited the deducted amount)

To download it: log in to incometax.gov.in → e-File → Income Tax Returns → View Form 26AS → Redirect to TRACES → Download as PDF or text.

One important thing to understand: Form 26AS is only as good as what deductors have filed. If your employer filed a wrong TDS return — say, they applied the wrong PAN or made a challan error — your 26AS will reflect that wrong data. You cannot correct 26AS yourself. Only the deductor can correct it by filing a revised TDS return.

AIS: The Full-Spectrum Information Feed

The Annual Information Statement (AIS) is a far more ambitious document. It collects data from dozens of reporting entities under Section 285BB of the Income Tax Act and CBDT's Statement of Financial Transactions (SFT) framework. The sources include banks, post offices, mutual fund registrars (CAMS and KFintech), depositories (NSDL and CDSL), the Registrar of Properties, insurance companies, foreign exchange dealers, and overseas financial institutions reporting under FATCA/CRS.

What this means in practice: if you bought ₹50,000 worth of mutual fund units in a month, AIS will know. If you received ₹15,000 in dividend from an equity fund, AIS will know. If you registered a property purchase, AIS will know. If you sent ₹7 lakh overseas under LRS, AIS will know. None of these transactions would have appeared in old Form 26AS unless TDS was specifically deducted.

AIS is organized into the following information categories:

  • TDS/TCS Information — same as 26AS but more detailed
  • SFT Information — mutual fund transactions, securities purchases, deposits above ₹10 lakh in savings accounts
  • Payment of Taxes — advance tax and self-assessment tax
  • Demand and Refund — pending demands and issued refunds
  • Other Information — foreign remittances (Form 15CA/15CB), interest on income tax refund, salary reported by employer, rent received

To access AIS: log in to incometax.gov.in → Services → Annual Information Statement (AIS) → Download as PDF or JSON.

The most important quirk of AIS: it sometimes double-counts. A single dividend payment may appear both as reported by the company and as reported by your depository. A single mutual fund SIP may appear as twelve separate entries. This is why TIS exists.

TIS: The Ready-to-Use Summary

The Taxpayer Information Summary sits above AIS and is automatically derived from it. CBDT's system applies de-duplication rules, aggregates transactions of the same type, and presents the result in a format that maps directly to the schedules in your ITR. Where AIS is a raw transaction log, TIS is a cleaned summary by income head.

TIS shows you figures like: total salary income as reported by employer, total interest income from banks, total dividend received, total mutual fund redemption proceeds, total capital gains (sometimes broken by STCG and LTCG). These aggregated figures are what the e-filing portal uses to pre-fill your ITR when you click the "Pre-fill ITR" button.

Here is where taxpayers get confused: the pre-filled figures from TIS are not always correct. TIS inherits any errors in the underlying AIS data, and its de-duplication logic is automated and occasionally wrong. Always treat pre-filled ITR data as a starting draft, not a final answer.

Where Mismatches Actually Come From

Understanding why these documents disagree saves you from panicking when they do. The most common reasons:

1. Employer filed a revised TDS return after a correction. If your company's payroll team corrected a challan error or updated your salary figures mid-year, the revised data may appear in AIS before it propagates to TRACES and Form 26AS. The two can lag each other by weeks or even months.

2. Bank or mutual fund house reported transactions at different aggregation levels. A bank may report your FD interest as a single annual figure, while AIS shows individual quarterly accruals. The total may be the same, but the presentation differs.

3. AIS double-counts the same transaction from two reporters. Dividends from listed companies are reported by both the company and the registrar (e.g., Link Intime or KFintech). If AIS has received both reports, it may show the dividend twice — TIS is supposed to de-duplicate this, but doesn't always succeed.

4. High-value transactions flagged under SFT that were not income. If you deposited ₹12 lakh in cash in your savings account (perhaps from selling a vehicle), AIS will flag it as a high-value cash deposit. This is not income — but it will appear in AIS as a reportable transaction and needs to be addressed in your ITR with an explanation of the source.

5. Old TDS wrongly mapped to your PAN. If someone else's PAN was incorrectly entered by a deductor, that TDS credit might land in your 26AS. Conversely, your legitimately deducted TDS may not show up if the deductor made a PAN entry error. Both situations require the deductor to file a correction.

The Reconciliation Process: Step by Step

Do this in this exact order before you start filling your ITR:

Step 1 — Download all three documents for the relevant financial year. Log in to incometax.gov.in. Download Form 26AS from TRACES (under e-File menu). Download AIS and TIS from the AIS section under Services. Keep them open side by side, ideally in separate tabs.

Step 2 — Verify TDS credits in 26AS against your actual documents. Take your salary slips, Form 16 (Part A and Part B), bank interest certificates, and rent receipts. For every TDS entry in Form 26AS — the employer's TAN, the deducted amount, the quarter — verify it against your source document. If a TDS credit that your employer told you about is missing from 26AS, contact your payroll team immediately; the TDS may not have been deposited.

Step 3 — Go through AIS income by income head. In AIS, open each income category: salary, interest, dividend, mutual fund, capital gains, foreign remittances. For each entry, note whether you recognise the transaction, whether the amount matches your records, and whether the transaction is yours at all. Flag anything that looks incorrect, inflated, or completely unfamiliar.

Step 4 — Compare AIS to TIS. For each income head, check whether TIS's aggregated figure matches what you calculated by adding up AIS entries. If TIS shows ₹38,000 in dividend but you can count only ₹34,000 across AIS entries, there is a logic gap — either TIS has included something AIS doesn't display at your view level, or TIS has under-aggregated. Note every discrepancy.

Step 5 — Build your own income reconciliation sheet. Take a blank spreadsheet or even paper. Create rows for each income head: salary, other sources (interest, dividends), capital gains (STCG, LTCG), exempt income. Populate each row with three columns: what your personal documents say, what TIS says, and the difference. The goal is to explain every rupee of difference before you open the ITR form.

Step 6 — Submit feedback on AIS for any incorrect entries. For each entry in AIS that is wrong — not yours, duplicated, or showing wrong amount — click "Feedback" on that entry and select the appropriate option from the dropdown. This creates a formal record of your objection. Note that feedback submission does not change TIS immediately; TIS updates may take a few days to reflect your feedback.

Step 7 — File the ITR with your actual correct figures. Your ITR should reflect your actual income as supported by source documents — not blindly copied from TIS. If TIS shows higher income than you actually earned due to an AIS error you've flagged, file with the correct figure and be prepared to explain the discrepancy if asked.

What to Do When You Find an Error

If the error is in 26AS (TDS amount wrong, credit missing): Contact the deductor — your employer's payroll team, your bank's TDS department, or whoever deducted the tax. Provide them your PAN and the specific quarter. They need to file a revised TDS return (Form 24Q for salary, 26Q for others) with TRACES. Once the revised return is processed, 26AS updates automatically. This can take 7–15 working days.

If the error is in AIS (transaction not yours, amount wrong, duplicate entry): Submit feedback directly on the AIS portal as described in Step 6. Keep a screenshot of your feedback for your records. If the error is a duplicate, mark it as "Income is included in other entry." If the transaction is not yours at all, mark it as "Income is not mine" and provide the reason.

If your TDS is shown but not deposited (Part G of 26AS): This is serious. It means the deductor cut TDS from your income but did not deposit it with the government. You cannot claim that TDS credit in your ITR. Raise the issue immediately with the deductor. If they are unresponsive, you can file a grievance on the TRACES portal or approach the assessing officer.

Timing matters: If you find errors late, you may have to file your ITR with a note explaining the discrepancy and wait for a processing-level query or minor intimation under 143(1). As long as you have filed with correct figures and your feedback is on record, most routine mismatches resolve without penalty.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which document should I trust if Form 26AS and AIS show different TDS amounts?

Neither blindly. Download both, identify the exact line-item difference, and cross-check with your salary slips or bank interest certificates. Always report in your ITR what you actually received and had tax deducted on — then raise a feedback on the AIS portal to flag any error so the tax department has your version on record.

What is the difference between AIS and TIS?

AIS is the comprehensive raw feed — it shows every transaction the Income Tax Department received information about under your PAN. TIS is a cleaned, consolidated summary derived from AIS that shows the net taxable value after removing duplicates and aggregating similar transactions. TIS is the more directly usable document for ITR filing, but you should always verify TIS figures against the underlying AIS entries.

Is Form 26AS still relevant after AIS was launched?

Yes. Form 26AS remains the primary TDS certificate document and is still used by banks, lenders, and visa authorities as official proof of tax deducted. AIS has more information but Form 26AS is the legally established document under Section 203AA. Check both before filing — AIS for comprehensive income coverage, 26AS as the official TDS record.

What should I do if I find an error in my AIS?

Submit feedback directly on the AIS portal on the Income Tax e-filing website. For each incorrect transaction, select the relevant feedback option such as "Income is not mine," "Income is included in other entry," or "Income is already included in ITR." The department reviews this feedback before processing your return. Filing feedback does not automatically correct the AIS, but it puts your objection on record.

What happens if I ignore a mismatch between my ITR and AIS?

The Income Tax Department's systems automatically compare your ITR against AIS. Significant mismatches — typically above ₹50,000 in unreported income — may trigger a notice under Section 143(1)(a). Larger or repeated mismatches can escalate to scrutiny assessment under Section 143(2). It is far easier to reconcile before filing than to respond to notices after.

The Bottom Line: Reconcile First, File Once

The three-document system exists because the Income Tax Department now collects financial information from dozens of sources — and it expects your ITR to be consistent with all of them. The filers who get notices are rarely people who committed fraud. They are mostly people who filed accurately from their own documents but didn't check what the department already knew from banks, brokers, and registrars. Spending one hour reconciling Form 26AS, AIS, and TIS before filing can save you six months of notice responses afterward.

Pull all three documents together every year. Build the reconciliation habit. And when you find a discrepancy — flag it, explain it, and file with the correct figure regardless of what AIS says. Your source documents are always your primary defence.
