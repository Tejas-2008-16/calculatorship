Direct Answer: Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal deposit and the accumulated interest from prior compounding cycles. In India, bank Fixed Deposits (FDs) compound quarterly, while Public Provident Fund (PPF) calculates monthly interest and compounds annually.

The Compound Interest Formula Explained

Compound interest is often referred to as "interest on interest." Unlike simple interest where your earnings remain constant year after year, compound interest adds your earned interest back into the principal pool at the conclusion of each conversion period.

A = P * (1 + r / n) ^ (n * t)

Where:

  • A: Total final maturity amount (Principal + Accumulated Interest).
  • P: Initial principal amount deposited.
  • r: Annual nominal interest rate expressed in decimal form (e.g. 7.5% = 0.075).
  • n: Number of compounding periods per year (Quarterly n=4; Annually n=1; Monthly n=12).
  • t: Total tenure in years.

2 Worked Mathematical Examples in Rupees

Example 1: Bank Fixed Deposit with Quarterly Compounding

You deposit ₹5,00,000 in a 5-year bank FD at 7.25% p.a. with quarterly compounding (mandated RBI standard for Indian scheduled commercial banks).

  • P = ₹5,00,000, r = 0.0725, n = 4, t = 5
  • Rate per quarter (r/n) = 0.0725 / 4 = 0.018125
  • Total conversion periods (n*t) = 4 * 5 = 20 quarters
  • Multiplier = (1 + 0.018125)^20 = (1.018125)^20 = 1.43166
  • Maturity = ₹5,00,000 * 1.43166 = ₹7,15,830
  • Total Compound Interest = ₹7,15,830 - ₹5,00,000 = ₹2,15,830

Under simple interest, the payout would have been only ₹1,81,250. Compounding yields an extra ₹34,580 in pure interest.

Example 2: Sovereign Gold Bond (SGB) / Corporate Bond (10 Years)

You invest ₹10,00,000 in a high-rated corporate infrastructure bond paying 8.50% p.a. compounding annually for 10 years.

  • P = ₹10,00,000, r = 0.085, n = 1, t = 10
  • Multiplier = (1.085)^10 = 2.26098
  • Maturity = ₹10,00,000 * 2.26098 = ₹22,60,980
  • Total Compound Interest = ₹12,60,980 (your interest exceeds your original deposit)

Who This Is NOT For and When It Is a Bad Idea

  • Do not apply fixed compound interest to volatile equity funds: Stocks and equity mutual funds do not compound at a fixed interest rate. Their growth fluctuates with market conditions. Use CAGR or XIRR for equities.
  • High tax bracket investors seeking wealth creation: Interest on fixed compounding instruments (FDs, corporate bonds) is added directly to your taxable income and taxed at your slab rate (up to 30% + surcharge + cess). After tax and 6% inflation, real returns on standard bank FDs are often zero or slightly negative.

3 Common Mistakes When Planning Compound Interest

1. Confusing Compounding Frequency with Deposit Frequency

Bank Fixed Deposits compound quarterly even if payout is cumulative at the end. PPF calculates monthly on the lowest balance between the 5th and end of the month, but compounds annually on March 31st.

2. Overlooking TDS under Section 194A

Banks deduct 10% TDS if cumulative FD interest exceeds ₹40,000 in a financial year (₹50,000 for senior citizens). If you fail to submit Form 15G/15H, TDS deducted directly reduces your compounding principal.

3. Early Premature Withdrawal Penalties

Withdrawing a cumulative FD before maturity breaks the compounding curve and triggers a penalty (typically 0.5% to 1.0% interest reduction on the run rate), drastically shrinking final payouts.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

How often do Indian banks compound FD interest?

By RBI guidelines, Indian banks compound Fixed Deposit interest quarterly (every 3 months: March, June, September, and December). Special short-term deposits below 91 days may follow simple interest.

Is compound interest on PPF tax-free?

Yes. Public Provident Fund operates under the Exempt-Exempt-Exempt (EEE) tax regime where the principal, compounded interest, and maturity withdrawal are 100% tax-free under Section 10(11).

What is the Rule of 72?

The Rule of 72 is a mental shortcut to estimate how many years it takes for your money to double with compound interest: divide 72 by the annual interest rate. At 8% interest, your capital doubles in approximately 9 years (72 / 8 = 9).
