Section 80TTA vs 80TTB: Which Interest Exemption Actually Applies to You

Under the Old Tax Regime, Section 80TTA allows individual taxpayers under age 60 and HUFs a tax deduction of up to ₹10,000 on savings bank account interest, excluding fixed and recurring deposits. Section 80TTB allows resident senior citizens (aged 60 and above) a much broader deduction of up to ₹50,000 across all interest income, including savings, fixed deposits, and recurring deposits. Neither deduction is permitted under the default New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC).

Every year, thousands of Indian taxpayers claim the wrong interest exemption - non-seniors assume their FD interest is covered under Section 80TTA, and senior citizens forget that 80TTB replaces 80TTA entirely once they turn 60. Here is exactly which section applies to you, what it does and does not cover, and how the math works under both tax regimes for FY 2026-27.

Key Takeaways: What You Need to Know First

  • 1. Different instruments entirely: Section 80TTA (₹10,000 limit) covers only savings account interest. Section 80TTB (₹50,000 limit) covers savings account interest AND fixed/recurring deposit interest combined.
  • 2. Age decides, not choice: Anyone below 60 must use 80TTA. Anyone 60 or above must use 80TTB - the two are never available together to the same person.
  • 3. FD interest gets zero benefit under 80TTA: A non-senior citizen's fixed deposit interest is fully taxable at slab rate with no exemption of any kind.
  • 4. Old Regime only: Both sections are completely disallowed under the default New Tax Regime for FY 2026-27.
  • 5. TDS threshold ≠ tax exemption: Banks skipping TDS on FD interest below ₹50,000/₹1,00,000 does not make that interest tax-free - it still must be reported and taxed in your ITR.

The Mix-Up That Costs Taxpayers Real Money

Ask ten salaried Indians what Section 80TTA covers, and at least six will tell you it exempts "bank interest" up to ₹10,000 - full stop, no further questions. That answer is half right and half expensive. By design, Section 80TTA exempts interest earned specifically on savings bank accounts. Savings deductions never covered fixed deposits, recurring deposits, or corporate bonds, and it never will under current law. Taxpayers who lump their FD interest into that ₹10,000 figure and skip reporting the rest are simply under-reporting income - something that shows up the moment the bank's TDS return doesn't match their ITR.

A second common mix-up runs the other way. Senior citizens sometimes assume they can claim the old ₹10,000 80TTA benefit on top of the newer ₹50,000 80TTB benefit, treating them as stackable. They are not. Once you qualify as a senior citizen, Section 80TTB takes over completely and Section 80TTA is no longer available to you, even though it covers a narrower category of interest.

Rules for 80TTA: The ₹10,000 Savings-Only Exemption

Section 80TTA applies to individuals below 60 years of age and to Hindu Undivided Families (HUFs). Taxpayers can claim a deduction of up to ₹10,000 per financial year on interest earned from savings accounts held with:

  • Any scheduled or co-operative bank
  • A post office savings account
  • A co-operative society carrying on banking business

The deduction applies to the aggregate savings interest across all your accounts, not per account. If you hold four savings accounts at four different banks and earn a combined ₹14,000 in interest, you can claim ₹10,000 as deduction and the remaining ₹4,000 gets added to your taxable income under "Income from Other Sources." Critically, this section makes no mention of fixed deposits, recurring deposits, or any time-locked instrument - that interest is fully taxable at your slab rate from the first rupee, with no threshold at all.

Rules for 80TTB: The ₹50,000 Senior Citizen Exemption

Section 80TTB was introduced specifically for resident senior citizens - anyone aged 60 years or above at any time during the financial year. Replacing 80TTA entirely for that person, it offers a much wider and larger benefit: a deduction of up to ₹50,000 per financial year on interest income from:

  • Savings bank accounts
  • Fixed deposits (including tax-saving FDs and Senior Citizen FD schemes)
  • Recurring deposits
  • Post office time deposits and post office savings schemes

This is the major upgrade for retirees. Unlike Section 80TTA, Section 80TTB explicitly covers FD and recurring deposit interest. That is where most seniors hold their savings. Note that the Rs 50,000 limit is a combined ceiling across all your banks and post offices under one PAN. All statutory ceilings apply in aggregate across institutions, not per bank account.

Why TDS Non-Deduction Doesn't Mean Tax-Free

Under Section 194A, banks deduct 10% TDS on FD interest once it crosses ₹50,000 in a financial year for non-seniors and ₹1,00,000 for senior citizens (60+) (per bank branch/CIF, aggregated across branches of the same bank). Many depositors see no TDS line on their interest certificate and conclude the amount is tax-free. Far from it: Section 194A thresholds govern solely whether the bank withholds tax at source - it has no bearing on whether the interest is taxable in your hands. Every rupee of FD interest, TDS or no TDS, must be reported under "Income from Other Sources" and taxed at your applicable slab rate, subject only to the 80TTA or 80TTB exemption where it genuinely applies.

If your total tax liability for the year is nil or below the TDS threshold, you can avoid the TDS deduction itself (not the underlying tax liability) by filing Form 15G (non-seniors) or Form 15H (senior citizens) with the bank at the start of the financial year - but only if you are genuinely eligible, meaning your total taxable income for the year is expected to be below the basic exemption limit.

Three Worked Examples

Illustrative example, not a real person: Names such as Rahul, Priya, Arun, Ananya, Vikram, Neha, Rohan, Suresh, Sneha, or Ramesh below refer to hypothetical calculation scenarios.

Example 1: Non-senior with savings interest only. Rohan, age 32, earns ₹8,200 in savings account interest across two banks and holds no FDs. Under Section 80TTA, the entire ₹8,200 is exempt since it is below the ₹10,000 ceiling. His taxable interest income is ₹0.

Example 2: Non-senior with FD interest. Priya, age 29, earns ₹6,500 in savings interest and ₹42,000 in FD interest from a 3-year fixed deposit. For Priya, Section 80TTA exempts the ₹6,500 savings interest fully (within the ₹10,000 cap, with ₹3,500 of unused limit that cannot be applied to FD interest). Her entire ₹42,000 FD interest is added to taxable income and taxed at her slab rate. Zero tax relief applies on that FD portion.

Example 3: Senior citizen with mixed interest. Suresh, age 67, earns ₹18,000 in savings account interest and ₹58,000 in Senior Citizen FD interest, totaling ₹76,000. Under Section 80TTB, he can claim a deduction of up to ₹50,000 against this combined total. His taxable interest income after exemption is ₹76,000 − ₹50,000 = ₹26,000, added to his total income and taxed at slab rate. He cannot also claim Rs 10,000 under Section 80TTA. Once you turn 60, Section 80TTA is completely off the table.

What Happens Under the New Tax Regime

For FY 2026-27, the New Tax Regime under Section 115BAC remains the default option unless you actively opt for the Old Regime while filing. Both Section 80TTA and Section 80TTB sit under Chapter VI-A deductions, and Chapter VI-A is almost entirely blocked under the New Regime - the same way Section 80C and 80D are blocked. In practice, choosing the New Tax Regime means your entire savings and FD interest - every rupee of it - gets added to your taxable income with no ₹10,000 or ₹50,000 cushion at all. Senior citizens with substantial FD interest income are usually the group hit hardest by this rule, since the Old Regime's ₹50,000 exemption plus the higher basic exemption limit for seniors can meaningfully lower their effective tax outgo compared to the New Regime. Before locking in a regime for the year, run both scenarios through a tax calculator using your actual interest income figures.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a senior citizen claim both Section 80TTA and Section 80TTB in the same year?

No. Both statutory sections are mutually exclusive for a senior citizen. Once you qualify as a senior citizen (60 years or above) at any point during the financial year, Section 80TTB applies to your entire interest income and Section 80TTA is no longer available to you for that year.

Does Section 80TTA cover fixed deposit interest?

No. Savings interest qualifies under Section 80TTA only when held with banks, co-operative societies, or post offices, up to ₹10,000 per year. Interest from fixed deposits, recurring deposits, and corporate bonds is fully taxable and gets no benefit under 80TTA.

Are 80TTA and 80TTB available under the New Tax Regime for FY 2026-27?

No. Both Section 80TTA and Section 80TTB fall under Chapter VI-A and are disallowed under the New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC). Under the New Regime, your entire savings and FD interest is added to taxable income with no separate exemption.

If my bank did not deduct TDS on FD interest, is that interest tax-free?

No. TDS non-deduction and tax exemption are unrelated. Banks skip TDS under Section 194A only when interest stays below the threshold (₹50,000 for non-seniors, ₹1,00,000 for seniors) or a valid Form 15G/15H is filed. All accrued interest remains fully taxable and must be reported in your ITR regardless of TDS.

Is the ₹50,000 limit under Section 80TTB per bank or a combined total?

Statutory limits apply as a combined aggregate ceiling. A senior citizen must add up savings account interest and FD/RD interest from every bank, post office, and co-operative society across their PAN, and the total exemption available under Section 80TTB cannot exceed ₹50,000 per financial year.

Actionable Next Step: Pull Your Annual Information Statement (AIS)

Log into the Income Tax e-filing portal and download your AIS. AIS reports every bank's submitted interest against your PAN - savings and FD combined. Add up the savings interest separately from the FD interest, apply the correct section based on your age, and compare the resulting tax liability under both the Old and New Regime before you file. Reconciling these entries prevents the most common notice triggers: under-reported interest income that doesn't match what banks have already told the department.
