TCS on LRS: Section 206C(1G) Tax on Overseas Remittances, Forex Cards & Foreign Education Payments in India (FY 2026-27)

When transferring funds overseas for education, medical treatment, overseas travel, or foreign investments, Indian residents encounter Tax Collected at Source (TCS) under Section 206C(1G). This guide outlines statutory rate schedules, the ₹7 Lakh annual threshold mechanics, Form 26AS/AIS credit verification, and ITR reconciliation procedures.

TCS is Not a Final Tax — It is an Advance Credit

TCS collected on LRS remittances is not a permanent charge or penalty. It is collected as an advance against your total income tax liability and appears in your Form 26AS. You can set it off in full when filing your annual ITR. If your total tax liability is less than TCS collected, the excess is refunded by the Income Tax Department.

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • General LRS TCS Rate: 20% on remittances exceeding ₹7 Lakh per financial year (investments abroad, gifts, maintenance, travel packages).
  • Education / Medical TCS: 5% on self-funded foreign education or medical treatment above ₹7 Lakh per year.
  • Education Loan TCS: 0.5% on overseas education funded through a financial institution loan — the most concessional rate.
  • Credit Card Exemption: International credit card transactions abroad are currently NOT under LRS TCS. Only formal bank wire transfers and forex card top-ups through authorized dealers attract TCS.
  • Claim It Back: Every rupee of TCS deducted shows up in Form 26AS and can be claimed as a credit against your tax liability in your ITR — it is recoverable as a refund if you have zero or lower tax.

1. What is the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS)?

The Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS) is a framework established by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) that allows Indian resident individuals to remit money abroad freely — without requiring prior RBI approval — up to a cumulative limit of USD 250,000 per financial year (approximately ₹2.1 Crore at current exchange rates).

LRS covers a wide range of cross-border money movements by resident Indians:

  • Current Account Transactions: Foreign travel expenses, overseas education fees, medical treatment abroad, maintenance of close relatives living overseas, gifts and donations to foreign entities, and purchase of foreign goods and services.
  • Capital Account Transactions: Investment in overseas stocks and bonds (see our detailed guide on Tax on US Stocks in India & Schedule FA), acquisition of immovable property abroad, opening and maintaining foreign currency accounts, making foreign direct investments, and setting up joint ventures abroad.

LRS was introduced in 2004 and has undergone several revisions in the annual limit over the years. It replaced a more restrictive regime where virtually every outward remittance required explicit RBI permission. The USD 250,000 cap is an annual limit that resets on April 1 each financial year. LRS applies only to resident individuals — NRIs operating through NRE or FCNR accounts use separate RBI frameworks and are not subject to the LRS TCS rules discussed here.

2. What is TCS on LRS and Which Law Governs It?

TCS stands for Tax Collected at Source. Unlike TDS (Tax Deducted at Source, which is deducted by the payer before credit), TCS is collected by the seller or service provider from the buyer at the time of the transaction and deposited with the government on the buyer's behalf.

Section 206C(1G) of the Income Tax Act, 1961 specifically mandates TCS on remittances made under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme. It was introduced by the Finance Act, 2020 — originally at a 5% rate — and subsequently revised significantly upward by the Finance Act, 2023 for most LRS categories, effective October 1, 2023.

The party responsible for collecting and remitting the TCS to the government is the Authorized Dealer (AD) — which is typically your bank (SBI, HDFC, ICICI, Axis, etc.) or an authorized money changer through whom you process the LRS remittance. The TCS is collected from you, the remitter, at the time the foreign exchange transaction is processed.

Overseas Education — Funded from Own Sources ₹7 Lakh per FY 5%
Medical Treatment Abroad ₹7 Lakh per FY 5%
All Other LRS Purposes — Investments, Gifts, Maintenance, Family Support, etc. ₹7 Lakh per FY 20% — General Rate

Note: If the remitter does not furnish PAN, the TCS rate doubles to 40% for most categories. Always ensure your PAN is registered and linked with your authorized dealer bank. Rates reflect the Finance Act, 2023 provisions effective October 1, 2023, continuing through FY 2026-27.

4. The ₹7 Lakh Annual Threshold: How It Works

The ₹7 Lakh threshold is a per-financial-year aggregate limit on LRS remittances before TCS kicks in. This threshold applies cumulatively across all remittances made for the same purpose through the same authorized dealer during the financial year.

Here is how it works in practice:

  • If you remit ₹5 Lakh abroad in June for investment in US stocks: No TCS (below ₹7 Lakh).
  • If you then remit another ₹4 Lakh in September (same FY, same authorized dealer): TCS at 20% applies only on ₹2 Lakh (the amount by which the cumulative ₹9 Lakh crosses the ₹7 Lakh threshold). TCS = ₹40,000.
  • Any further remittances in the same FY will attract full 20% TCS with no threshold deduction (threshold is already exhausted).

An important nuance: the ₹7 Lakh threshold is tracked by each authorized dealer separately for their own customers. If you make remittances through two different banks, each bank tracks its own cumulative total against the threshold for its own transactions. This does not mean you can split remittances across banks to game the threshold — the Income Tax Department has visibility across all transactions through your PAN, and the aggregate LRS exposure is reported in your Annual Information Statement (AIS).

Overseas Tour Package — No ₹7 Lakh Threshold

Overseas tour packages sold by Indian tour operators are a special carve-out. TCS at 5% applies from the very first rupee up to ₹7 Lakh in the package cost, and at 20% on the portion of the package price exceeding ₹7 Lakh. There is no zero-TCS zone for tour packages — every holiday package payment attracts TCS immediately.

5. Which Transactions Attract LRS TCS?

LRS TCS applies to outward remittances processed through authorized dealers under the RBI's LRS framework. The most common transactions that attract TCS include:

  • Wire transfers to foreign bank accounts — sending money to your own account abroad, to a child studying overseas, to a relative living in another country, or to a foreign vendor.
  • Forex card top-ups — loading foreign currency onto a multi-currency travel card at a bank or authorized money changer for international travel.
  • International travel packages — purchasing overseas holiday packages from Indian tour operators (TCS from ₹1, no threshold).
  • Overseas education fees — paying tuition fees directly to a foreign university from an Indian bank account.
  • Foreign medical treatment — remitting money for treatment at overseas hospitals.
  • Overseas stock/ETF investments — investing in US stocks through platforms like INDmoney, ICICI Direct Global, or similar that route purchases through LRS.
  • Foreign real estate purchases — remitting purchase consideration for overseas property.

Transactions That Do NOT Attract LRS TCS

  • International credit card transactions made directly abroad (not currently under LRS).
  • Remittances made under separate RBI frameworks that fall outside LRS (e.g., NRI NRO/NRE account transfers under FEMA specific provisions).
  • Remittances for imports of goods and services made by businesses under current account liberalization rules outside LRS.
  • Amounts up to the ₹7 Lakh annual threshold for education, medical, and general LRS purposes.

6. How TCS is Collected: Role of Authorized Dealers

Authorized Dealers (ADs) — typically banks and authorized money changers licensed by the RBI — are legally obligated under Section 206C(1G) to collect TCS from the remitter at the time the foreign exchange transaction is processed. The process works as follows:

  1. You initiate the remittance — either at a bank branch, through net banking, or via a SWIFT transfer request.
  2. The authorized dealer verifies cumulative LRS — checks the remittances already processed for you in the current financial year, compares against the ₹7 Lakh threshold, and determines the TCS amount.
  3. TCS is collected from you — added to the total amount you pay, or deducted from the remittance amount, depending on the bank's process. In practice, you are informed of the TCS amount at the time of the transaction.
  4. TCS is deposited with the government — the authorized dealer deposits the TCS to the Central Government using your PAN within the prescribed due dates.
  5. TCS appears in your Form 26AS / AIS — the collected TCS is reflected in your Form 26AS under the "Tax Collected at Source" section, with the authorized dealer's TAN as the collector. This is available for download from the Income Tax e-filing portal.

7. How to Claim TCS Credit in Your ITR

Claiming TCS credit when filing your Income Tax Return is straightforward. Here is the step-by-step process:

  1. Download your Form 26AS from the Income Tax e-filing portal (incometax.gov.in) — go to "View Form 26AS" under e-file → Income Tax Returns. Verify that all TCS amounts appear correctly with the authorized dealer's TAN.
  2. Cross-reference with AIS — also check your Annual Information Statement (AIS) for TCS entries. AIS captures data from multiple sources and is increasingly detailed.
  3. File your ITR (ITR-2 for most cases) — if you have LRS remittances, you are likely also reporting capital gains (foreign investments) or foreign income, which would require ITR-2. In ITR-2, go to Schedule TCS (Tax Collected at Source) and enter the TCS details from Form 26AS.
  4. TCS auto-populates — if you use the pre-filled ITR on the e-filing portal, TCS entries from Form 26AS often pre-populate automatically. Verify the figures before submission.
  5. TCS is credited against your tax liability — the system computes your total tax payable, then deducts TDS + TCS + advance tax paid. Any excess becomes a refund; any shortfall is payable as self-assessment tax.

Illustrative Example: TCS Credit in ITR

  • Resident Indian remits: ₹12,00,000 abroad for US stock investments in FY 2026-27.
  • LRS TCS deducted by bank: 20% on ₹5,00,000 (the amount above ₹7 Lakh threshold) = ₹1,00,000 TCS.
  • Investor's total income tax liability (from salary, business income, capital gains): ₹3,50,000.
  • TDS on salary already deducted: ₹2,20,000.
  • Net tax computation: ₹3,50,000 (liability) − ₹2,20,000 (TDS) − ₹1,00,000 (TCS credit) = ₹30,000 self-assessment tax to pay.
Result: The ₹1,00,000 TCS was not a loss — it reduced the investor's final tax payment from ₹1,30,000 to ₹30,000. No refund required in this case, but the TCS was fully utilized.

8. Worked Numerical Examples

Example 1: Parent Sending Money for Child's Overseas Education (Own Funds)

  • Amount remitted: ₹30,00,000 (₹30 Lakh) to a UK university
  • Funding source: Parent's own savings (no education loan)
  • Applicable TCS rate: 5% above ₹7 Lakh
  • Taxable amount: ₹30,00,000 − ₹7,00,000 = ₹23,00,000
  • TCS deducted: 5% × ₹23,00,000 = ₹1,15,000
  • This ₹1,15,000 appears in Form 26AS and can be claimed as credit in the parent's ITR.

Example 2: Parent Sending Money for Child's Overseas Education (Education Loan)

  • Amount remitted: ₹30,00,000 to a UK university — funded via an education loan from an Indian bank
  • Applicable TCS rate: 0.5% above ₹7 Lakh
  • Taxable amount: ₹30,00,000 − ₹7,00,000 = ₹23,00,000
  • TCS deducted: 0.5% × ₹23,00,000 = ₹11,500
  • Saving vs own-funded route: ₹1,15,000 − ₹11,500 = ₹1,03,500 lower TCS — a substantial cash flow advantage for borrowers.

Example 3: Investing ₹15 Lakh in US Stocks Through a Broker

  • Amount remitted via LRS for US stock purchase: ₹15,00,000
  • Applicable TCS rate: 20% above ₹7 Lakh (general investment purpose)
  • Taxable amount: ₹15,00,000 − ₹7,00,000 = ₹8,00,000
  • TCS deducted: 20% × ₹8,00,000 = ₹1,60,000
  • Total outflow from investor's account: ₹15,00,000 + ₹1,60,000 = ₹16,60,000
  • ₹1,60,000 TCS is recoverable via ITR credit or refund — but it creates a significant short-term cash flow impact.

9. Forex Cards and International Credit Cards: TCS Applicability

Forex Cards (Multi-Currency Travel Cards)

When you load foreign currency onto a multi-currency forex card at a bank or authorized money changer, this is treated as an LRS remittance. If the cumulative amount loaded onto the card exceeds ₹7 Lakh in the financial year, TCS at 20% applies on the excess. For most Indian travelers using forex cards for a single international holiday, the total card load typically stays well under ₹7 Lakh, so TCS does not apply. Business travelers or frequent flyers who load large amounts should be aware of the cumulative LRS tracking.

International Credit Cards — Currently NOT Under LRS TCS

This has been a source of confusion since Budget 2023. The Finance Act 2023 initially proposed including international credit card transactions made abroad under the LRS/TCS framework. This was scheduled to take effect from July 1, 2023 and generated significant concern among frequent international travelers and business professionals.

However, the Government of India deferred the implementation of this provision following considerable pushback, pending further review. As of FY 2026-27, international credit card transactions made abroad (where you swipe your Indian Rupee credit card at an overseas merchant or website) are NOT subject to LRS TCS. These transactions continue to be settled through the card network and do not fall under the formal LRS route through an authorized dealer.

This means: if you book a Singapore Airlines ticket on the airline's website using your HDFC or SBI credit card, no TCS applies. If you instead wire the equivalent rupee amount to Singapore Airlines' account via an outward wire transfer, TCS would apply. The distinction is the payment method and channel — credit card vs bank wire transfer.

11. Frequently Asked Questions

If I have no tax liability, can I get the full TCS back as a refund?

Yes. If your total income tax liability for the financial year is zero (for example, your income is below the basic exemption limit or you have sufficient deductions), then the entire TCS deducted on your LRS remittances can be claimed as a refund by filing your ITR. The Income Tax Department will process the refund after ITR verification. This is why TCS on LRS creates a temporary cash flow inconvenience for low-income individuals who use LRS — they must wait for the annual ITR cycle to get the money back.

Does TCS apply if I send money to my spouse or child living abroad for their personal expenses?

Yes. Maintenance or gift remittances to family members abroad are classified as "general LRS" remittances. TCS at 20% applies on the cumulative amount exceeding ₹7 Lakh per financial year. There is no exemption for family maintenance transfers under LRS TCS — the rate is the same 20% regardless of the relationship or purpose. If your child is abroad for education, the specific education TCS rate of 5% (or 0.5% with an education loan) applies only to payments made directly as tuition or education fees to an educational institution.

Is the ₹7 Lakh LRS threshold the same as the ₹7 Lakh income threshold under the New Tax Regime?

No — these are completely unrelated. The ₹7 Lakh income threshold under the New Tax Regime refers to the Section 87A tax rebate that makes income up to ₹7 Lakh effectively tax-free for residents. The ₹7 Lakh LRS threshold under Section 206C(1G) refers to the annual cumulative amount of outward foreign remittances below which no TCS is levied. They happen to share the same number but serve completely different purposes and have no interaction with each other.

How do I prove that my LRS remittance was for education via a loan, to get the lower 0.5% TCS rate?

You must provide documentary evidence to the authorized dealer (bank) processing the remittance. The key document is the education loan sanction letter from the financial institution (scheduled bank, financial institution, or approved charitable institution under Section 80E). The loan must be a formal loan from an institution qualifying under Section 80E — informal loans from friends or family do not qualify for the 0.5% rate. Submit the loan sanction letter and university admission letter to your bank before initiating the remittance.

How to Think About This Yourself

  • Liquidity Buffer for TCS: Because TCS on non-education foreign remittances above ₹7 Lakh is 20%, ensure your cash flow accommodates this advance payment until you reconcile it on your annual tax return.
  • Cross-Check AIS & Form 26AS: Authorized Dealer banks report TCS quarterly. Verify that the collecting bank has credited your PAN accurately before finalizing your ITR.
  • Documentation for Education Rates: Maintain bank loan sanction letters under Section 80E and university admission letters to substantiate concessional 0.5% or 5% remittance claims.
