Arbitrage Mutual Funds in India: Cash-Futures Spread Mechanics & Equity Taxation

I'm Tejas, a student. Arbitrage mutual funds generate returns by capturing temporary price differences between cash (spot) shares and stock futures, while maintaining SEBI's 65% equity allocation rule. Understanding their mechanics, yield drivers, and tax classification clarifies how they function relative to debt instruments and bank deposits.

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • Core Mechanism: Arbitrage funds simultaneously buy stocks in the cash market and sell equal quantities in the stock futures market, capturing the locked price differential upon monthly expiry.
  • Low Directional Risk: Because the position is completely hedged, whether the stock index climbs 5% or crashes 10% before expiry, the net return remains fixed.
  • The Tax Loophole: By holding at least 65% in equity and equity derivatives, the fund is legally classified as an equity fund, attracting 20% STCG or 12.5% LTCG instead of a 30% slab rate.
  • Ideal Horizon: Best suited for parking surplus capital for 3 months to 3 years, emergency fund tier-2/tier-3 reserves, or parking bonus payouts before executing an STP into equity.
  • Exit Load Window: Most schemes charge a 0.25% exit load if redeemed within 15 to 30 days, making them unsuitable for emergency cash needed within 48 hours.

1. What Is an Arbitrage Mutual Fund?

In standard financial theory, the term arbitrage refers to the simultaneous purchase and sale of an identical asset in two distinct markets to profit from a price discrepancy. In India's capital markets, equity shares trade on two parallel venues: the spot (cash) market and the derivative (futures) market of the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

Under ordinary market circumstances, a stock's futures contract trades at a slight premium over its spot cash price. This difference is known as the "cost of carry," which reflects the financing interest cost and dividend expectations between today and the expiry date of the futures contract on the last Thursday of each month.

An Arbitrage Mutual Fund is an open-ended hybrid mutual fund scheme whose primary investment mandate is to capture this cost-of-carry spread continuously. Rather than speculating on whether Reliance Industries, Infosys, or HDFC Bank will move higher or lower, the fund manager locks in the price spread the moment the trade is executed.

2. How Cash-Futures Arbitrage Works: A Concrete Example

To grasp why directional stock market movements do not hurt an arbitrage fund's NAV, let us trace a realistic trade step by step:

  • Date: 1st of the month
  • Stock: ABC Limited
  • Cash Market Price: ₹1,000 per share
  • Current Month Futures Price: ₹1,006 per share
  • Gross Spread: ₹6 per share (0.60% for a 30-day holding period, annualizing to approximately 7.20%)

The fund manager executes two trades at the exact same second:

  1. Buys 1,000 shares of ABC Limited in the cash market at ₹1,000 each (Cost: ₹10,00,000).
  2. Sells (Shorts) 1 futures contract (equivalent to 1,000 shares) of ABC Limited at ₹1,006 each (Contract value: ₹10,06,000).

On the final expiry day of the derivative contract, the spot cash price and the futures contract price must legally converge to the exact same value. Consider both extreme outcomes on expiry day:

Scenario Cash Market Leg Futures Market Leg Net Realized Profit
Bull Market: Stock rallies to ₹1,100 Gain: +₹1,00,000 (sold at ₹1,100) Loss: -₹94,000 (squared at ₹1,100) +₹6,000 Profit
Bear Market: Stock crashes to ₹900 Loss: -₹1,00,000 (sold at ₹900) Gain: +₹1,06,000 (squared at ₹900) +₹6,000 Profit

In both scenarios, whether the underlying stock surges 10% or plummets 10%, the net return is exactly ₹6,000. The market direction has zero impact on the outcome. The fund manager then rolls over the position into the next month's contract or squares off and identifies new arbitrage spreads.

3. SEBI Regulations: The 65% Portfolio Allocation Rule

Under the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) mutual fund categorization framework issued under circular SEBI/HO/IMD/DF3/CIR/P/2017/114, an Arbitrage Fund is defined as an open-ended scheme following an arbitrage strategy with the following statutory portfolio constraints:

  • Minimum Investment in Equity & Equity-Related Instruments: 65% of total assets.
  • Hedging Strategy: The equity exposure must be predominantly hedged through completely offsetting derivative contracts (stock and index futures).
  • Debt and Money Market Instruments: Up to 35% of total assets can be held in high-grade short-term debt instruments, Treasury Bills, Triparty Repos (TREPS), and Certificate of Deposits (CDs) to maintain liquidity and earn baseline interest.

This 65% threshold is the cornerstone of the entire product. Even though the equity exposure is hedged and carries near-zero directional market risk, holding 65% gross equity qualifies the scheme as an "equity-oriented fund" under Section 112A and Section 111A of the Income Tax Act, 1961.

4. The Equity Taxation Advantage: STCG & LTCG Rules

The landmark change in the Finance Act, 2023 amended the taxation of pure debt mutual funds. Since 1 April 2023, capital gains from mutual funds holding less than 35% equity are taxed as regular income at the investor's applicable slab rate, entirely stripping away indexation benefits.

Because Arbitrage Funds maintain at least 65% in equity and derivatives, they escaped this tax overhaul completely. Following the updated tax rates enacted under Finance (No. 2) Act, 2024, the tax treatment for Indian resident investors is:

Holding Period Classification Tax Rate (FY 2026-27) Exemptions / Conditions
Up to 12 Months Short-Term Capital Gains (STCG) 20% + 4% cess = 20.80% Taxed under Section 111A. No basic exemption slab adjustment unless resident total income is below basic limit.
More than 12 Months Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) 12.5% + 4% cess = 13.00% Taxed under Section 112A. First ₹1.25 Lakh of aggregate equity LTCG across all funds/stocks is 100% tax-free each year.

For an individual in the 30% or 39% surcharge income brackets, paying 20% on short-term gains (or 12.5% after 1 year with a ₹1.25 Lakh exemption) represents a substantial tax saving compared to paying 30% to 39% on Bank FD interest or Liquid Fund gains.

6. Worked Math: Post-Tax Returns Across Income Slabs

Let us look at a numerical scenario. An investor parks ₹10,00,000 for exactly 12 months. Assume both the Arbitrage Fund and a 1-year Bank Fixed Deposit generate a pre-tax gross return of 7.00% (₹70,000 gain):

Tax Bracket Bank FD Post-Tax Yield Arbitrage Fund Post-Tax Yield Net In-Hand Advantage
Nil (Income below ₹7L – ₹12L rebate) 7.00% (₹70,000) 7.00% (₹70,000) Nil (Parity)
20% Slab Rate 5.54% (₹55,440) 7.00% (₹70,000)* +₹14,560 extra
30% Slab Rate (31.2% effective with cess) 4.82% (₹48,160) 7.00% (₹70,000)* +₹21,840 extra

*Note: If held for over 12 months, the ₹70,000 gain falls entirely within the annual ₹1,25,000 LTCG tax-free threshold (Section 112A), resulting in ₹0 tax. Even if redeemed within 12 months, the 20% STCG tax rate results in an in-hand return of ₹55,440, still outperforming the 30% slab FD by over ₹7,280.

7. Real Risks and Limitations Most Investors Overlook

While arbitrage mutual funds are significantly safer than traditional equity funds, calling them "risk-free" is factually incorrect. Here are the four key operational risks:

  1. Spread Compression in Dull Markets: When equity markets turn sluggish, retail participation in derivatives drops and futures trading premiums shrink. During these phases, gross arbitrage spreads can dip to 4.5% to 5.5%, lowering returns below liquid funds temporarily.
  2. Debt Portfolio Credit Risk: Up to 35% of an arbitrage fund is invested in debt and money market securities. If an AMC holds lower-rated commercial papers, any credit event or default in that debt portfolio can directly cause NAV drawdowns. Market observers often examine fact sheets to verify credit quality and sovereign/AAA allocation in the debt sleeve.
  3. Short-Term Exit Loads: Redeeming within 15 to 30 days incurs an exit load (typically 0.25%). This makes arbitrage funds unsuitable as immediate overnight emergency cash.
  4. Daily NAV Fluctuations: Because futures contracts are marked-to-market daily, an arbitrage fund's NAV will not show a straight uninterrupted line like a liquid fund. Over a 1-week window, returns can be slightly negative, though over 30 to 90 days they smoothen out as derivatives approach monthly expiry.

8. How Investors Evaluate Holding Periods and Use-Cases

In financial planning literature, arbitrage funds are typically analyzed across specific use-cases and horizons:

  • Tax Slab Considerations: For individuals in higher income tax brackets (such as 30%), the equity taxation framework (20% STCG / 12.5% LTCG) results in lower tax incidence than debt funds or fixed deposits taxed at slab rates.
  • Tier-2 Cash Reserves: While immediate 1-month expenses are often held in savings accounts or liquid instruments, secondary reserves are sometimes parked in arbitrage funds where a 3- to 6-month horizon allows exit loads to lapse.
  • Systematic Transfer Staging: When deploying a lump sum into equity over time via a Systematic Transfer Plan (STP), an arbitrage fund is frequently used as the source pool because it offers equity taxation and lower volatility than pure equity.
  • Holding Horizon Dynamics: Financial educators note that holding periods under 15–30 days incur exit loads, while holding over 12 months allows gains to qualify for LTCG treatment under Section 112A.

How to think about this yourself

  • Tax Bracket Math: Calculate your post-tax yield. If your slab rate is 10% or 15%, bank FDs or treasury bills may offer comparable post-tax returns with sovereign backing or DICGC cover. At the 30% slab, equity taxation math changes the net yield significantly.
  • Horizon vs Exit Load: If you need money within 15–30 days, exit loads and daily mark-to-market variations make liquid or overnight funds more mathematically appropriate.
  • Debt Sleeve Quality: Check the scheme's monthly fact sheet to verify the proportion of sovereign, AAA, and cash equivalents in the non-equity sleeve.

9. Frequently Asked Questions

Can Arbitrage Funds give negative returns?

Over daily or weekly periods, yes — mark-to-market fluctuations in derivatives and debt paper can produce small negative returns. However, over rolling 3-month and 6-month horizons, well-managed arbitrage funds holding high-quality debt have historically tended to smooth out because price convergence at derivative expiry captures the spread.

Are Arbitrage Funds covered by DICGC bank deposit insurance?

No. DICGC insurance of ₹5 Lakh applies exclusively to commercial and co-operative bank deposits. Arbitrage Funds are SEBI-regulated mutual funds and carry market-linked risk with no government capital guarantee.

Do I have to pay Securities Transaction Tax (STT) on Arbitrage Funds?

Yes. Because they are classified as equity-oriented mutual funds, an STT of 0.001% is deducted automatically by the AMC at the time of unit redemption. This charge is factored into the net redemption payout.
