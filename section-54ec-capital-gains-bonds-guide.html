Section 54EC Capital Gains Bonds: Rules, Limits & Tax Exemption

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • Exclusive to Real Estate: Under the amended Section 54EC, exemption is strictly applicable to long-term capital gains arising from land, building, or both. Equity or gold gains do not qualify.
  • ₹50 Lakh Annual Ceiling: An investor can claim a maximum deduction of ₹50,00,000 per financial year under Section 54EC(1).
  • Strict 6-Month Timeline: The bond subscription must be executed within 6 months from the date of the property transfer (sale deed date). The Section 139(1) ITR filing due date is irrelevant for 54EC investments.
  • 5-Year Lock-in: Bonds issued on or after April 1, 2018 have a mandatory 5-year lock-in. Pledging, mortgaging, or taking a loan against these bonds revokes the exemption retroactively.
  • Taxable Interest: The coupon rate (around 5.25% p.a.) is non-cumulative and fully taxable under Other Sources at your applicable slab rates.

1. What is Section 54EC of the Income Tax Act?

When an individual, Hindu Undivided Family (HUF), or corporate taxpayer transfers a long-term capital asset, the resulting profit is subject to capital gains tax. While long-term capital gains tax on unlisted immovable assets is levied at statutory rates, the Income Tax Act provides specific relief mechanisms to mitigate this tax burden.

Section 54EC was formulated to incentivize infrastructure financing by allowing taxpayers to invest their net long-term capital gains into notified government-backed infrastructure bonds. In return, the amount invested—up to a statutory ceiling of ₹50,00,000—is deducted directly from the taxable capital gains, reducing the investor's tax liability to zero on that portion.

2. Eligible Assets & Post-2018 Statutory Scope

Prior to the Finance Act 2018, taxpayers could invest long-term capital gains arising from any capital asset—including residential property, commercial shops, agricultural land, shares, mutual funds, or gold jewellery—into 54EC bonds.

However, an essential amendment restricted the definition of the original asset under Section 54EC. Today, the asset sold must strictly satisfy two conditions:

  • Asset Nature: It must be a long-term capital asset being land or building or both (immovable property). This encompasses residential plots, agricultural land located within notified urban municipal limits, residential flats, commercial office spaces, and industrial sheds.
  • Holding Period: The immovable property must have been held for more than 24 months immediately preceding the date of transfer to qualify as long-term.

Gains from financial assets, such as equity shares, mutual funds, or debt securities, cannot be shielded using Section 54EC bonds under current tax provisions.

3. Authorised Issuers, Coupon Rates & Lock-in Terms

Section 54EC specifies that investments must be made only in "long-term specified assets" issued by central public sector enterprises notified by the Central Government. The four designated entities eligible to issue 54EC capital gains bonds are:

  • REC Limited (formerly Rural Electrification Corporation Limited)
  • PFC Limited (Power Finance Corporation Limited)
  • NHAI (National Highways Authority of India)
  • IRFC (Indian Railway Finance Corporation)

4. Statutory Limits: The ₹50 Lakh Ceiling & 6-Month Deadline

Two non-negotiable legal restrictions govern Section 54EC investments:

The Exact 6-Month Investment Window

The statute requires that the investment into capital gains bonds must be completed within a period of 6 months after the date of such transfer. For real estate transactions, the date of transfer is normally the date on which the registered conveyance or sale deed is executed and possession is delivered, not the date when earnest money or booking advance was received.

Unlike Section 54 (which permits parking unutilized capital gains in a Capital Gains Account Scheme before the Section 139(1) tax filing due date), Section 54EC does not permit any extension via the Capital Gains Account Scheme. If the 6 months elapse without bond allocation, the entire tax exemption is permanently forfeited.

The ₹50 Lakh Statutory Cap per Taxpayer

Section 54EC(1) caps the total permissible investment at ₹50,00,000. Earlier, some taxpayers attempted to double this limit by selling property between October and March and investing ₹50 Lakh before March 31, followed by another ₹50 Lakh in April (spanning two financial years within the 6-month window). The Finance Act explicitly introduced the second proviso to Section 54EC, clarifying that the aggregate investment made from capital gains arising in a single financial year cannot exceed ₹50 Lakh, regardless of whether the investment spans across two financial years.

5. Worked Real Estate Capital Gains Case Study

To examine the practical tax mechanics, let us simulate a real-world scenario of an Indian property sale during FY 2026-27:

Simulation: Sale of an Urban Residential Plot

  • Original Acquisition Date: May 14, 2012
  • Sale Deed Execution Date: August 10, 2026
  • Gross Sale Consideration: ₹1,20,00,000
  • Indexed Cost of Acquisition / Base Cost: ₹48,00,000
  • Net Computed Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG): ₹72,00,000

Option A: Zero Tax Planning (Pay Full LTCG)

Without claiming deductions under Section 54 or Section 54EC, the entire ₹72,00,000 gain is taxable. At a baseline 12.5% rate (plus 4% cess and applicable surcharge), the taxpayer incurs an immediate tax liability of approximately ₹9,36,000 to ₹10,80,000 depending on total income.

Option B: Strategic Section 54EC Allocation

The taxpayer subscribes to 500 units of REC / PFC Section 54EC bonds on or before February 9, 2027 (within 6 months of sale):

  • Amount Invested in 54EC Bonds: ₹50,00,000
  • Section 54EC Exemption Claimed: ₹50,00,000
  • Remaining Net Taxable LTCG: ₹72,00,000 − ₹50,00,000 = ₹22,00,000
  • Direct Capital Gains Tax Saved: ₹50,00,000 × 12.5% = ₹6,25,000 (plus saved surcharge and cess, exceeding ₹6,50,000 in cash preserved).
  • Annual Cash Flow Received: ₹50,00,000 × 5.25% = ₹2,62,500 annual pre-tax interest coupon throughout the 5-year lock-in period.

7. Crucial Operational Traps & Loan Restrictions

Taxpayers investing in Section 54EC bonds must remain alert to several specific compliance conditions:

  • The Loan / Pledge Prohibition: Under Section 54EC(2), if the taxpayer converts the bonds into cash, sells them, or takes any loan, advance, or overdraft against the security of the bonds before the 5-year lock-in expires, the entire capital gain previously exempted is treated as taxable long-term capital gain of the financial year in which such encumbrance occurred. Banks are strictly instructed not to mark liens or approve loans against 54EC instruments.
  • Payment Clearance Timing: The bond application and cheque clearing process can take 7 to 10 days. Waiting until the final week of the 6-month deadline risks bank settlement delays, which could push the effective allotment beyond the statutory 6-month limit.
  • Demat vs Physical Format: While bonds can be held in physical certificate form, opting for Demat mode expedites electronic credit of the annual coupon and prevents paperwork friction at the end of the 5-year maturity term.
  • Reporting in Schedule CG: The exemption is not automatically granted; it must be formally claimed in ITR-2 or ITR-3 by entering the bond allotment details, date of investment, and issuer details under the deductions table of Schedule CG.

8. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I invest in 54EC bonds if I sell shares or mutual funds?

No. Effective April 1, 2018, Section 54EC applies exclusively to long-term capital gains originating from land or buildings. It cannot be used to offset gains from equity shares or mutual funds.

What happens if the property sale yields ₹80 Lakh LTCG?

You can invest up to the statutory ceiling of ₹50,00,000 in Section 54EC bonds. The remaining ₹30,00,000 capital gain can either be reinvested in a residential house under Section 54 (if eligible) or paid as capital gains tax.

Is TDS deducted on 54EC bond interest payments?

No TDS is deducted by REC, PFC, NHAI, or IRFC on interest paid to resident individual bondholders under Section 193. However, the interest income must be declared in your tax return under 'Income from Other Sources' and taxed at your applicable slab rate.

Can co-owners each invest ₹50 Lakh in Section 54EC bonds?

Yes. If a property is jointly owned by two individuals (e.g. husband and wife) and both are co-sellers named on the deed, each co-owner is assessed separately on their respective share of capital gains. Both can independently invest up to ₹50 Lakh in 54EC bonds, shielding up to ₹1 Crore in total gains across the joint property.
