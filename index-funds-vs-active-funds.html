Index Funds vs Active Mutual Funds in India: SPIVA Research and Expense Ratio Arithmetic

Hi, I'm Tejas. I'm an engineering student researching personal finance structures in India. This guide analyzes how passive index funds and actively managed equity funds operate, reviewing published SPIVA India Scorecard data and the mathematical impact of Total Expense Ratio (TER) differentials. This is strictly educational research and arithmetic—it is not investment advice or a recommendation to buy either fund type. Consult a SEBI-registered Investment Adviser (RIA) for personalized portfolio guidance.

Key Concepts: Passive vs Active Framework

  • Index funds are passive vehicles designed to mirror the composition and return profile of a target benchmark index (e.g., Nifty 50 or BSE Sensex) with rule-based rebalancing and low Total Expense Ratios (TER).
  • Actively managed funds rely on fund managers and research analysts to select securities with the objective of generating returns in excess of a benchmark (alpha), incurring higher ongoing management fees.
  • The SPIVA (S&P Indices Versus Active) India Scorecard evaluates how active Indian mutual funds perform relative to their respective category benchmarks across 1, 3, 5, and 10-year holding periods.
  • SEBI's 2017 categorisation circular strictly defines asset limits (e.g., Large-Cap funds must invest at least 80% in top 100 stocks), impacting the ability of active managers to deviate from benchmark constituents in liquid segments.
  • Fee differentials compound mathematically over long holding periods, requiring active funds to generate sufficient gross excess returns to offset higher management expenses.

The comparative evaluation of index funds versus actively managed mutual funds centers on the relationship between cost, market efficiency, and active management risk. In passive investing, an investor accepts the return of the market index minus a small expense ratio and tracking error. In active investing, an investor pays higher management fees in pursuit of potential benchmark outperformance (alpha), while taking on manager selection and style drift risk.

The SPIVA India Scorecard: Empirical Performance Data

The S&P Indices Versus Active (SPIVA) India Scorecard periodically evaluates the percentage of active Indian equity mutual funds that underperform their benchmarks after all fees and expenses. In the 2024 Scorecard for the 10-year period ending December 2024:

  • In the Indian Large-Cap category, 84% of actively managed funds underperformed the S&P BSE 100 / Nifty 100 TRI over a 10-year horizon.
  • In the ELSS (Tax Saving) category, 77% of active funds underperformed the S&P BSE 200 over 10 years.
  • In the Indian Mid-Cap and Small-Cap categories, 51% of active funds underperformed their category benchmarks over 10 years, indicating a roughly equal split between outperforming and underperforming managers.

Financial researchers attribute these variations to differing degrees of market efficiency: heavily traded blue-chip companies are widely analyzed by institutional market participants, making information discovery faster, whereas mid and small-cap segments exhibit wider dispersions in individual company performance.

To examine the impact of expense ratios on accumulated corpus, consider an illustrative ₹10,000 monthly SIP across multiple hypothetical compounding rates over a 20-year horizon (total invested principal ₹24 Lakh), comparing a typical Direct Index Fund (~0.15% TER) against a Direct Active Fund (~0.95% TER):

Market Capitalization and Research Coverage in India

The operational environment for active portfolio management varies across market capitalization segments:

  • Large-Cap Segment (Top 100 Companies): Subject to extensive institutional scrutiny, high foreign and domestic analyst coverage, and regulatory investment constraints under SEBI norms (minimum 80% allocation to large caps).
  • Mid-Cap and Small-Cap Segments (Rank 101 onwards): Characterized by lower average analyst coverage, wider earnings variability, and larger performance dispersion across individual companies, which can create opportunities for active security selection.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does holding both an index fund and a large-cap active fund provide meaningful diversification?

Portfolio holdings analysis often shows significant overlap (frequently 60% to 75%) between large-cap active funds and benchmark indices like the Nifty 50 or Nifty 100, because both draw from the same universe of top 100 stocks. Investors review portfolio overlap to ensure they are not duplicating holdings while paying higher active fees.

Do index funds provide downside protection during market downturns?

No. An index fund is designed to replicate its benchmark index directly, absorbing market declines in tandem with the index. Actively managed funds retain the mandate to hold cash or shift sector allocations, though historical data shows that market timing during corrections remains challenging for active managers.

Portfolio Architecture Concepts: Core-Satellite Framework

Some institutional investors and financial planners examine a Core-Satellite architecture: allocating a foundational portion of equity investments into low-cost, broad-market index funds (Core) while allocating secondary portions to active strategies or specialized categories (Satellite) where alpha potential is sought. This structure balances systematic market beta with selective active management.

How to Think About This Yourself

The choice between index funds and active mutual funds is not binary. Consider your conviction in active manager selection, the market capitalization segment you are accessing, and your sensitivity to ongoing management fees. Review scheme factsheets for tracking error (for index funds) and rolling benchmark alpha (for active funds).
