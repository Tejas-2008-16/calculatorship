Section 89(1) Tax Relief & Form 10E: Complete Guide for Salary Arrears, DA Arrears, Gratuity & Lump Sum Income

Written by Tejas Waghmare Published: 4 October 2026 Last reviewed: 23 November 2026 Salary Taxation: Section 89(1) Relief, Rule 21A 6-Step Math & Mandatory Form 10E

Educational tax relief guide only. Not personal chartered accountancy advice. Online submission of Form 10E on the e-filing portal is mandatory to claim Section 89 relief. Read our full disclaimer.

Section 89(1) of the Income-tax Act provides statutory tax relief when past salary arrears, DA arrears, or lump sum gratuity received in a single financial year artificially push an employee into a higher tax bracket. Computed under Rule 21A, the relief equals the excess tax payable on arrears in the year of receipt minus the tax that would have been payable had the arrears been received in the past financial year(s) they relate to. To claim this relief, filing online Form 10E on the Income Tax e-filing portal before submitting your ITR is legally mandatory; failure to file Form 10E results in automatic rejection and an automated tax demand notice.

However, the relief is not automatic. Statutory rules mandate filing Form 10E electronically on the e-filing portal before submitting your ITR. Claiming Section 89(1) relief without an electronic Form 10E on record, CPC Bengaluru disallows the deduction and issues a demand notice under Section 143(1). Below is the complete mathematical model and form-filing walkthrough.

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • Purpose: Section 89(1) prevents unfair over-taxation when salary arrears, DA arrears, gratuity, or VRS compensation is received in a lump sum in one financial year instead of spread across the years it relates to.
  • Mechanism: The relief equals the difference between (a) tax actually payable when the arrear/lump sum is added to current year income, and (b) what tax would have been due if the income had been received in the correct years.
  • Mandatory Filing: Form 10E must be filed online at incometax portal before submitting your ITR, with zero exceptions.
  • Both Regimes: Section 89(1) relief is available under both the Old Tax Regime and the New Tax Regime.
  • Most Common Use Case: Government employees receiving DA arrears from a pay commission revision, an adjustment claimed by millions every few years.

1. Why Section 89(1) Exists: The Bunching Problem

India's income tax system taxes income on a "year of receipt" basis for salary income. Arrears create a structural tax problem when earnings relating to previous years is received in a single current year, creating a phenomenon tax professionals call "income bunching."

Illustrative example, not a real person: Suppose Ravi is a salaried employee whose regular salary is ₹12,50,000 per year (within FY 2026-27's ₹12.75 Lakh Section 87A zero-tax limit). In April 2026, a pay revision pays Ravi ₹5,50,000 in arrears covering two prior financial years. Without Section 89(1):

  • FY 2026-27 gross income: ₹12,50,000 (current salary) + ₹5,50,000 (arrears) = ₹18,00,000
  • FY 2026-27 New Regime tax on ₹18,00,000 (after ₹75,000 standard deduction): ₹1,50,800 (including 4% cess), compared to ₹0 on his regular ₹12,50,000 salary alone
  • Tax Ravi would have paid if arrears arrived on time: Spreading ₹2,75,000 across each prior year when total income fell in lower marginal brackets yields a noticeably lower combined tax bill.

Higher tax liabilities in the year of receipt stem purely from timing of payment, not any genuine increase in annual wealth. Statutory relief under Section 89(1) neutralizes bracket creep by computing what Ravi's tax would have been if the income had been received in the years it actually related to, and then granting the difference as relief.

2. Who Can Claim Section 89(1) Relief?

Section 89(1) relief is available to any individual taxpayer (resident or non-resident) who receives qualifying income in a financial year where the income relates to one or more prior financial years, resulting in a higher aggregate tax than would have been due had the income been received in those prior years.

Prominent claimant categories include:

  • Government employees receiving DA arrears from central or state government pay commission revisions, which form the single largest category of Section 89(1) claimants in India.
  • Salaried employees in the private sector who receive salary revision arrears (backdated pay hikes covering multiple previous months or years).
  • Retiring employees receiving gratuity, commuted pension, or VRS/severance compensation that exceeds the tax-exempt limits.
  • Employees receiving advance salary, representing a lump sum paid in advance for future months (less common, but equally eligible).

The relief is not available if your income in the earlier year(s) was higher than in the current year; in that case, spreading the income back would have resulted in more tax, not less, and Section 89(1) does not create additional tax liability, only a reduction.

3. Types of Income Eligible for Section 89(1) Relief

Section 89 read with Rule 21A of the Income Tax Rules, 1962 specifies the following categories of income eligible for relief:

Category of Income Rule Under Rule 21A Relevant Form 10E Table
Salary / Wages received in arrears or in advance Rule 21A(2) Table A
Gratuity (taxable portion above exemption) Rule 21A(3) Table B
Commuted value of pension Rule 21A(4) Table C
Retrenchment compensation (taxable portion) Rule 21A(5) Table D
Voluntary Retirement Compensation / VRS (taxable portion above ₹5 Lakh exemption) Rule 21A(5A) Table E

DA arrears specifically fall under Table A (salary received in arrears). When you receive DA arrears covering multiple years in one go, you fill out Table A in Form 10E (with the exact form filing process detailed in a later section).

4. How Section 89(1) Relief is Calculated: Step-by-Step Method

The calculation follows a specific methodology prescribed in Rule 21A. For salary arrears (Table A), the 8-step process works as follows:

  1. Step 1: Compute the tax on your total income of the current financial year including the arrears to compute Tax(A).
  2. Phase 2: Compute the tax on your total income of the current financial year excluding the arrears to determine Tax(B).
  3. Phase 3: Compute the difference: Tax(A) − Tax(B). Such amounts represent the additional tax burden created by receiving arrears in the current year.
  4. Stage 4: For each previous year to which the arrear relates, take the income that was received in that year (excluding the arrear) and add the portion of the arrear that relates to that year. Compute the tax on this notional total income, designated as Tax(C_n) for each prior year n.
  5. Stage 5: Compute the tax on the actual income of each prior year (without the arrear), designated as Tax(D_n) for each prior year n.
  6. Step 6: Compute the additional tax that would have been payable in each prior year if the arrear had been received in that year: Tax(C_n) − Tax(D_n) for each year n.
  7. Step 7: Sum up all the prior year additional taxes: Sum of [Tax(C_n) − Tax(D_n)]. Historical calculations reflect the extra tax that would have been due if the income had been received on time.
  8. Step 8: The Section 89(1) relief = Step 3 minus Step 7 = [Tax(A) − Tax(B)] − [Sum of (Tax(C_n) − Tax(D_n))]. Positive balances qualify directly for tax relief. Zero or negative figures (meaning spreading the income would have caused more tax in prior years), no relief is granted.

Calculations proceed year by year for each affected prior year the arrear relates to. Online e-filing utilities handle these calculations automatically once you enter the income figures for each year.

5. Worked Example: DA Arrears Received in FY 2026-27

Scenario: Government Employee DA Arrears (2 Prior Years)

Illustrative example, not a real person: Anita is a salaried employee earning ₹14,00,000 regular salary in FY 2026-27. In June 2026, she receives ₹4,00,000 in arrears equally covering FY 2023-24 (₹2,00,000) and FY 2024-25 (₹2,00,000). She stays in the New Tax Regime throughout:

FY 2026-27 (Year of Receipt — ₹75,000 Std Ded)

  • Gross salary (excl. arrears): ₹14,00,000 (₹13.25L taxable) → Tax incl. 4% cess: ₹81,900
  • Total gross income with ₹4,00,000 arrears: ₹18,00,000 (₹17.25L taxable) → Tax incl. 4% cess: ₹1,50,800
  • Additional tax due to arrears [Step 3]: ₹1,50,800 − ₹81,900 = ₹68,900

FY 2023-24 (Notional: ₹8,50,000 + ₹2,00,000 = ₹10,50,000; Std Ded ₹50,000)

  • Tax on actual ₹8,50,000 (FY 23-24 New Regime rates, incl. 4% cess): ₹36,400
  • Tax on notional ₹10,50,000 (incl. 4% cess): ₹62,400
  • Additional tax for FY 23-24 [Step 6]: ₹62,400 − ₹36,400 = ₹26,000

FY 2024-25 (Notional: ₹9,50,000 + ₹2,00,000 = ₹11,50,000; Std Ded ₹75,000)

  • Tax on actual ₹9,50,000 (FY 24-25 New Regime rates, incl. 4% cess): ₹39,000
  • Tax on notional ₹11,50,000 (incl. 4% cess): ₹63,700
  • Additional tax for FY 24-25 [Step 6]: ₹63,700 − ₹39,000 = ₹24,700

Section 89(1) Relief Calculation

  • Step 7 (sum of historical additional taxes): ₹26,000 + ₹24,700 = ₹50,700
  • Section 89(1) Relief = ₹68,900 − ₹50,700 = ₹18,200
Result: Anita's tax liability on the arrears drops from ₹68,900 to ₹50,700, generating an exact saving of ₹18,200.

These exact slab figures assume salaried standard deduction and 4% cess under each year's New Regime schedule; use the official Form 10E portal utility for final filing values.

6. Worked Example: Salary Revision Arrears (Private Sector)

Scenario: Private Sector Salary Revision Arrears

Illustrative example, not a real person: Kiran works at a private company. His salary was revised in April 2026 from ₹10 Lakh to ₹14 Lakh per annum with effect from April 2025. His employer pays ₹4,00,000 in salary arrears (covering April 2025 to March 2026) in May 2026, together with his regular FY 2026-27 salary.

  • FY 2026-27 regular salary: ₹14,00,000
  • Arrears received: ₹4,00,000 (relating entirely to FY 2025-26)
  • Total FY 2026-27 income: ₹18,00,000, attracting much higher marginal tax than expected

Kiran can claim Section 89(1) relief by showing that in FY 2025-26, his total income would have been ₹10,00,000 + ₹4,00,000 = ₹14,00,000, which would have been taxed at significantly lower marginal rates than adding ₹4,00,000 to an already ₹14,00,000 salary in FY 2026-27.

In this scenario, the tax saving from Section 89(1) relief can be substantial, potentially reaching ₹40,000 to ₹70,000 or more depending on the exact tax regime and slab calculations for both years. Kiran's employer's Form 16 will reflect the arrears, and the ITR must include the Section 89(1) relief claim supported by Form 10E.

7. Form 10E: How to File It Online on the e-Filing Portal

Form 10E is the statutory declaration that must be filed on the Income Tax e-filing portal before submitting your ITR. Form 10E details the year-by-year income and tax breakdown that supports your Section 89(1) relief claim. System utilities perform the relief calculation automatically once you enter the figures.

Step-by-Step Form 10E Filing Process

  1. Login to incometax portal using your PAN and password.
  2. Navigate to e-File → Income Tax Forms → File Income Tax Forms.
  3. Search for "Form 10E" in the form list and select it.
  4. Select the Assessment Year for which you are claiming relief (for example, AY 2027-28 for FY 2026-27 income).
  5. Select the appropriate Table matching your income type:
    • Table A: Salary arrears / advance salary
    • Table B: Gratuity
    • Table C: Commuted pension
    • Table D: Retrenchment compensation
    • Table E: VRS compensation
  6. Enter the total income for each financial year: the portal provides rows for the current year and each prior year the arrear relates to.
  7. The portal automatically calculates the tax for each year using the correct slab rates for that assessment year and computes the Section 89(1) relief.
  8. Verify the computed relief amount and submit Form 10E by clicking Preview → Generate XML → Submit.
  9. After successful submission, download the acknowledgment of Form 10E; keep this for your records.
  10. Now proceed to file your ITR: the Section 89(1) relief amount from Form 10E should be entered in Schedule S (under salary income and relief) in your ITR.
Critical Rule: Form 10E Timeline

File Form 10E BEFORE you submit your ITR. Centralized processing systems check whether Form 10E has been filed before allowing Section 89(1) relief. Absence of Form 10E when your ITR is processed, the relief is automatically disallowed, even if you are genuinely entitled to it. There is a well-established body of tribunal orders confirming this requirement. Don't skip this step.

8. Section 89(1) Relief Under Old vs New Tax Regime

Section 89(1) relief is available under both the Old Tax Regime and the New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC). Arrear adjustments utilize tax slab rates applicable to the regime you have chosen in each relevant financial year.

A few nuances to keep in mind:

  • Consistency is not required: You may have been on the Old Regime in a prior year and switched to the New Regime in the current year. Calculations in Form 10E utilize applicable annual rates individually, including the correct regime chosen for that year.
  • Lower rates in New Regime: Under the New Regime, the slab rates are generally lower, especially for the ₹7 Lakh to ₹12 Lakh income range. In practice, the marginal tax that would have been due on arrears received in a prior New Regime year may be lower, reducing the "historical additional tax" figure and potentially increasing the Section 89(1) relief in some scenarios.
  • Prior-year income below exemption limit: If your income in a prior year (including the arrear notionally added) would still be below the basic exemption limit (₹2.5 Lakh for Old Regime, ₹3 Lakh for New Regime), the additional tax for that year is zero, meaning the full incremental tax in the current year that relates to that year's arrear becomes relief.

9. Common Mistakes That Cost You the Relief

  • Not filing Form 10E at all: The most common error. Many taxpayers simply enter the Section 89(1) relief amount in their ITR without realizing Form 10E must be separately filed. ITR claims face rejection without Form 10E on record.
  • Filing Form 10E after the ITR: If you file the ITR first and then realize you need Form 10E, the relief claimed in the original ITR is disallowed. You must file a Revised ITR after filing Form 10E, within the revision deadline.
  • Using wrong income figures: Form 10E requires the total gross income for each prior year, including all heads of income, not just salary. Using only the salary income figure for prior years will produce an incorrect (and usually over-stated) relief calculation that may be scrutinized in assessment.
  • Claiming relief for routine bonuses: Performance bonuses or annual bonuses paid in the current year for current-year performance do not qualify as "arrears" under Section 89(1). Relief only applies to payments that genuinely relate to prior years (specifically where you were entitled to the income in those years but received it later due to administrative delay or policy revision.
  • Double-claiming with employer: Some employers attempt to provide Section 89(1) relief in the Form 16 itself (reducing TDS at source). Whenever the employer has already computed and applied relief, you should not claim the same relief again in your ITR, though you must still file Form 10E to validate the employer's calculation. Always reconcile with your HR/payroll team.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

My employer has already deducted TDS accounting for Section 89(1) relief. Do I still need to file Form 10E?

Yes, you still need to file Form 10E. When an employer provides Section 89(1) relief in their TDS computation (reduced TDS deduction), they should ideally compute Form 10E internally to validate the relief. However, the obligation to file Form 10E on the e-filing portal lies with you, the employee, when you file your ITR. Your Form 16 will reflect the arrears and the relief granted by your employer. You must ensure Form 10E is filed on the e-filing portal with matching or correctly revised figures before you submit your ITR.

Is Section 89(1) relief available for professional income or business income received as arrears?

No. Section 89(1) relief is specifically available only for income taxable under the head "Salaries" (including profits in lieu of salary as defined in Section 17). Relief does not apply to professional fees, business income, rental income, or capital gains received in arrears. Only salary, wages, DA arrears, gratuity, commuted pension, retrenchment compensation, and VRS payments qualify.

What if I received salary arrears relating to a year when I had no other income: does Section 89(1) still help?

Yes, and it can produce the maximum possible relief. Where your income in the prior year (including the notional arrear portion) would have been below the basic exemption limit or attracted zero or minimal tax, then the "additional tax for that year" is zero. Under such rules, all incremental tax that the current year suffers because of that arrear becomes Section 89(1) relief. For example, if you were unemployed or a student in a prior year and received arrears for a job that started later, the relief can be very substantial.

Can I claim Section 89(1) relief for arrears received from a previous employer?

Yes. Relief under Section 89(1) applies to salary arrears regardless of whether the employer is your current employer or a previous employer. Where a court order, settlement, or administrative decision results in salary arrears being paid by a former employer in the current year, you can claim relief. The arrears should be reported in your ITR under salary income (consolidating Form 16 data from both employers if applicable), and Form 10E must be filed covering the years the arrears relate to.
