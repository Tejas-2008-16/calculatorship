Section 89(1) Tax Relief & Form 10E: Complete Guide for Salary Arrears, DA Arrears, Gratuity & Lump Sum Income

When salary arrears, DA arrears from a government pay revision, a large gratuity, or VRS compensation lands in your account in a single financial year, your tax liability for that year can spike sharply — not because you suddenly earned more, but because the tax system bundles years of income into one unfairly. Section 89(1) of the Income Tax Act exists precisely to fix this inequity. This guide explains who qualifies, how the relief is calculated step by step, and — critically — why filing Form 10E on the e-filing portal before you submit your ITR is non-negotiable.

Form 10E Must Be Filed BEFORE Your ITR

This is the single most important rule about Section 89(1) relief: if you claim relief in your ITR without filing Form 10E on the income tax e-filing portal first, the Income Tax Department will reject the claim and issue a deficiency notice. File Form 10E first. Then file your ITR. No exceptions.

Key Takeaways at a Glance

  • Purpose: Section 89(1) prevents unfair over-taxation when salary arrears, DA arrears, gratuity, or VRS compensation is received in a lump sum in one financial year instead of spread across the years it relates to.
  • Mechanism: The relief equals the difference between (a) tax actually payable when the arrear/lump sum is added to current year income, and (b) what tax would have been due if the income had been received in the correct years.
  • Mandatory Filing: Form 10E must be filed online at incometax.gov.in before submitting your ITR — no exceptions.
  • Both Regimes: Section 89(1) relief is available under both the Old Tax Regime and the New Tax Regime.
  • Most Common Use Case: Government employees receiving DA arrears from a pay commission revision — millions of employees claim this every few years.

1. Why Section 89(1) Exists: The Bunching Problem

India's income tax system taxes income on a "year of receipt" basis for salary income. This creates a structural problem when income relating to previous years is received in a single current year — a phenomenon tax professionals call "income bunching."

Consider a simple scenario: Ravi is a government employee who earns ₹8 Lakh per year. In April 2026, the government implements a pay commission revision and pays Ravi ₹5,40,000 in DA arrears covering the period from April 2024 to March 2026 (two full years). Without Section 89(1):

  • FY 2026-27 taxable income: ₹8,00,000 (current salary) + ₹5,40,000 (arrears) = ₹13,40,000
  • Tax on ₹13,40,000 (under New Tax Regime): approximately ₹1,71,200 plus cess
  • Tax Ravi would have paid if arrears arrived on time: Much lower — ₹2,70,000 additional salary per year for 2 years would have attracted far less marginal tax than getting all ₹5,40,000 in one year.

The higher tax in the year of receipt is purely a function of the timing of payment — not any genuine increase in annual wealth. Section 89(1) corrects this by computing what Ravi's tax would have been if the income had been received in the years it actually related to, and then granting the difference as relief.

2. Who Can Claim Section 89(1) Relief?

Section 89(1) relief is available to any individual taxpayer — resident or non-resident — who receives qualifying income in a financial year where the income relates to one or more prior financial years, resulting in a higher aggregate tax than would have been due had the income been received in those prior years.

The most common categories of claimants are:

  • Government employees receiving DA arrears from central or state government pay commission revisions — the single largest group of Section 89(1) claimants in India.
  • Salaried employees in the private sector who receive salary revision arrears (backdated pay hikes covering multiple previous months or years).
  • Retiring employees receiving gratuity, commuted pension, or VRS/severance compensation that exceeds the tax-exempt limits.
  • Employees receiving advance salary — a lump sum paid in advance for future months (less common, but equally eligible).

The relief is not available if your income in the earlier year(s) was higher than in the current year — in that case, spreading the income back would have resulted in more tax, not less, and Section 89(1) does not create additional tax liability, only a reduction.

3. Types of Income Eligible for Section 89(1) Relief

Section 89 read with Rule 21A of the Income Tax Rules, 1962 specifies the following categories of income eligible for relief:

Category of Income Rule Under Rule 21A Relevant Form 10E Table
Salary / Wages received in arrears or in advance Rule 21A(2) Table A
Gratuity (taxable portion above exemption) Rule 21A(3) Table B
Commuted value of pension Rule 21A(4) Table C
Retrenchment compensation (taxable portion) Rule 21A(5) Table D
Voluntary Retirement Compensation / VRS (taxable portion above ₹5 Lakh exemption) Rule 21A(5A) Table E

DA arrears specifically fall under Table A (salary received in arrears). When you receive DA arrears covering multiple years in one go, you fill out Table A in Form 10E — more on the exact form filing process in a later section.

4. How Section 89(1) Relief is Calculated: Step-by-Step Method

The calculation follows a specific methodology prescribed in Rule 21A. For salary arrears (Table A), the 8-step process works as follows:

  1. Step 1: Compute the tax on your total income of the current financial year including the arrears — this gives you Tax(A).
  2. Step 2: Compute the tax on your total income of the current financial year excluding the arrears — this gives you Tax(B).
  3. Step 3: Compute the difference: Tax(A) − Tax(B). This is the additional tax burden created by receiving the arrears in the current year.
  4. Step 4: For each previous year to which the arrear relates, take the income that was received in that year (excluding the arrear) and add the portion of the arrear that relates to that year. Compute the tax on this notional total income — call it Tax(C_n) for each prior year n.
  5. Step 5: Compute the tax on the actual income of each prior year (without the arrear) — call it Tax(D_n) for each prior year n.
  6. Step 6: Compute the additional tax that would have been payable in each prior year if the arrear had been received in that year: Tax(C_n) − Tax(D_n) for each year n.
  7. Step 7: Sum up all the prior year additional taxes: Sum of [Tax(C_n) − Tax(D_n)]. This represents the extra tax that would have been due historically if the income had been received on time.
  8. Step 8: The Section 89(1) relief = Step 3 minus Step 7 = [Tax(A) − Tax(B)] − [Sum of (Tax(C_n) − Tax(D_n))]. If this figure is positive, you get relief. If it is zero or negative (meaning spreading the income would have caused more tax in prior years), no relief is granted.

This calculation is performed year by year for each prior year the arrear relates to. The e-filing portal's Form 10E tool handles these calculations automatically once you enter the income figures for each year.

5. Worked Example: DA Arrears Received in FY 2026-27

Scenario: Government Employee — DA Arrears for 2 Prior Years

Anita is a Central Government employee. In June 2026, she receives DA arrears of ₹3,60,000 covering April 2024 to March 2026 (FY 2024-25 component: ₹1,80,000; FY 2025-26 component: ₹1,80,000). She uses the New Tax Regime throughout.

FY 2026-27 (Year of Receipt)

  • Gross salary (excl. arrears): ₹9,00,000
  • DA arrears received: ₹3,60,000
  • Total income with arrears: ₹12,60,000
  • Tax on ₹12,60,000 (New Regime): ₹1,02,700 approx (including 4% cess)
  • Tax on ₹9,00,000 (New Regime, excl. arrears): ₹41,600 approx
  • Additional tax due to arrears [Step 3]: ₹1,02,700 − ₹41,600 = ₹61,100

FY 2024-25 (Notional — Had ₹1,80,000 DA been received then)

  • Actual income in FY 2024-25: ₹7,50,000
  • Notional income with FY 24-25 portion of arrears: ₹7,50,000 + ₹1,80,000 = ₹9,30,000
  • Tax on ₹9,30,000 (New Regime FY 24-25 rates): ₹47,320 approx
  • Tax on ₹7,50,000 (actual, FY 24-25 rates): ₹31,200 approx
  • Additional tax for FY 24-25 [Step 6]: ₹47,320 − ₹31,200 = ₹16,120

FY 2025-26 (Notional — Had ₹1,80,000 DA been received then)

  • Actual income in FY 2025-26: ₹8,20,000
  • Notional income with FY 25-26 portion: ₹8,20,000 + ₹1,80,000 = ₹10,00,000
  • Tax on ₹10,00,000 (New Regime FY 25-26 rates): ₹54,600 approx
  • Tax on ₹8,20,000 (actual): ₹36,400 approx
  • Additional tax for FY 25-26 [Step 6]: ₹54,600 − ₹36,400 = ₹18,200

Section 89(1) Relief Calculation

  • Step 7 (sum of historical additional taxes): ₹16,120 + ₹18,200 = ₹34,320
  • Section 89(1) Relief = ₹61,100 − ₹34,320 = ₹26,780
Result: Anita's tax bill on the arrears drops from ₹61,100 to ₹34,320 — a saving of ₹26,780.

These are illustrative approximate numbers using simplified slab calculations. Actual numbers depend on the exact slab rates and surcharge for each financial year. Use the Form 10E portal calculator for precise figures.

6. Worked Example: Salary Revision Arrears (Private Sector)

Scenario: Private Sector Salary Revision Arrears

Kiran works at a private company. His salary was revised in April 2026 from ₹10 Lakh to ₹14 Lakh per annum with effect from April 2025. His employer pays ₹4,00,000 in salary arrears (covering April 2025 to March 2026) in May 2026, together with his regular FY 2026-27 salary.

  • FY 2026-27 regular salary: ₹14,00,000
  • Arrears received: ₹4,00,000 (relating entirely to FY 2025-26)
  • Total FY 2026-27 income: ₹18,00,000 — attracting much higher marginal tax than expected

Kiran can claim Section 89(1) relief by showing that in FY 2025-26, his total income would have been ₹10,00,000 + ₹4,00,000 = ₹14,00,000, which would have been taxed at significantly lower marginal rates than adding ₹4,00,000 to an already ₹14,00,000 salary in FY 2026-27.

In this scenario, the tax saving from Section 89(1) relief can be substantial — potentially ₹40,000 to ₹70,000 or more depending on the exact tax regime and slab calculations for both years. Kiran's employer's Form 16 will reflect the arrears, and the ITR must include the Section 89(1) relief claim supported by Form 10E.

7. Form 10E: How to File It Online on the e-Filing Portal

Form 10E is the statutory declaration that must be filed on the Income Tax e-filing portal before submitting your ITR. It contains the year-by-year income and tax breakdown that supports your Section 89(1) relief claim. The portal performs the relief calculation automatically once you enter the figures.

Step-by-Step Form 10E Filing Process

  1. Login to incometax.gov.in using your PAN and password.
  2. Navigate to e-File → Income Tax Forms → File Income Tax Forms.
  3. Search for "Form 10E" in the form list and select it.
  4. Select the Assessment Year for which you are claiming relief (e.g., AY 2027-28 for FY 2026-27 income).
  5. Select the appropriate Table matching your income type:
    • Table A — Salary arrears / advance salary
    • Table B — Gratuity
    • Table C — Commuted pension
    • Table D — Retrenchment compensation
    • Table E — VRS compensation
  6. Enter the total income for each financial year — the portal provides rows for the current year and each prior year the arrear relates to.
  7. The portal automatically calculates the tax for each year using the correct slab rates for that assessment year and computes the Section 89(1) relief.
  8. Verify the computed relief amount and submit Form 10E by clicking Preview → Generate XML → Submit.
  9. After successful submission, download the acknowledgment of Form 10E — keep this for your records.
  10. Now proceed to file your ITR — the Section 89(1) relief amount from Form 10E should be entered in Schedule S (under salary income and relief) in your ITR.
Critical Rule: Form 10E Timeline

File Form 10E BEFORE you submit your ITR. The Income Tax Department's processing system checks whether Form 10E has been filed before allowing Section 89(1) relief. If Form 10E is absent when your ITR is processed, the relief is automatically disallowed — even if you are genuinely entitled to it. There is a well-established body of tribunal orders confirming this requirement. Don't skip this step.

8. Section 89(1) Relief Under Old vs New Tax Regime

Section 89(1) relief is available under both the Old Tax Regime and the New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC). The relief calculation uses the tax slab rates applicable to the regime you have chosen in each relevant financial year.

A few nuances to keep in mind:

  • Consistency is not required: You may have been on the Old Regime in a prior year and switched to the New Regime in the current year. The Form 10E calculation uses the applicable rates for each year individually — including the correct regime chosen for that year.
  • Lower rates in New Regime: Under the New Regime, the slab rates are generally lower, especially for the ₹7 Lakh–₹12 Lakh income range. This means the marginal tax that would have been due on arrears received in a prior New Regime year may be lower, reducing the "historical additional tax" figure and potentially increasing the Section 89(1) relief in some scenarios.
  • If prior year income was below exemption limit: If your income in a prior year (including the arrear notionally added) would still be below the basic exemption limit (₹2.5 Lakh for Old Regime, ₹3 Lakh for New Regime), the additional tax for that year is zero — meaning the full incremental tax in the current year that relates to that year's arrear becomes relief.

9. Common Mistakes That Cost You the Relief

  • Not filing Form 10E at all: The most common error. Many taxpayers simply enter the Section 89(1) relief amount in their ITR without realizing Form 10E must be separately filed. The ITR claim is rejected without Form 10E on record.
  • Filing Form 10E after the ITR: If you file the ITR first and then realize you need Form 10E, the relief claimed in the original ITR is disallowed. You must file a Revised ITR after filing Form 10E, within the revision deadline.
  • Using wrong income figures: Form 10E requires the total gross income for each prior year — including all heads of income, not just salary. Using only the salary income figure for prior years will produce an incorrect (and usually over-stated) relief calculation that may be scrutinized in assessment.
  • Claiming relief for routine bonuses: Performance bonuses or annual bonuses paid in the current year for current-year performance do not qualify as "arrears" under Section 89(1). Relief only applies to payments that genuinely relate to prior years — i.e., where you were entitled to the income in those years but received it later due to administrative delay or policy revision.
  • Double-claiming with employer: Some employers attempt to provide Section 89(1) relief in the Form 16 itself (reducing TDS at source). If the employer has already computed and applied relief, you should not claim the same relief again in your ITR — though you must still file Form 10E to validate the employer's calculation. Always reconcile with your HR/payroll team.

10. Frequently Asked Questions

My employer has already deducted TDS accounting for Section 89(1) relief. Do I still need to file Form 10E?

Yes, you still need to file Form 10E. When an employer provides Section 89(1) relief in their TDS computation (reduced TDS deduction), they should ideally compute Form 10E internally to validate the relief. However, the obligation to file Form 10E on the e-filing portal lies with you, the employee, when you file your ITR. Your Form 16 will reflect the arrears and the relief granted by your employer. You must ensure Form 10E is filed on the e-filing portal with matching or correctly revised figures before you submit your ITR.

Is Section 89(1) relief available for professional income or business income received as arrears?

No. Section 89(1) relief is specifically available only for income taxable under the head "Salaries" (including profits in lieu of salary as defined in Section 17). It does not apply to professional fees, business income, rental income, or capital gains received in arrears. Only salary, wages, DA arrears, gratuity, commuted pension, retrenchment compensation, and VRS payments qualify.

What if I received salary arrears relating to a year when I had no other income — does Section 89(1) still help?

Yes, and it can produce the maximum possible relief. If your income in the prior year (including the notional arrear portion) would have been below the basic exemption limit or attracted zero or minimal tax, then the "additional tax for that year" is zero. This means the entire incremental tax that the current year suffers because of that arrear becomes Section 89(1) relief. For example, if you were unemployed or a student in a prior year and received arrears for a job that started later, the relief can be very substantial.

Can I claim Section 89(1) relief for arrears received from a previous employer?

Yes. Section 89(1) relief applies to salary arrears regardless of whether the employer is your current employer or a previous employer. If a court order, settlement, or administrative decision results in salary arrears being paid by a former employer in the current year, you can claim relief. The arrears should be reported in your ITR under salary income (consolidating Form 16 data from both employers if applicable), and Form 10E must be filed covering the years the arrears relate to.
