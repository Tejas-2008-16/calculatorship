Section 54F Capital Gains Exemption Guide: Invest Sale Proceeds to Save Tax

When Indian investors liquidate substantial portfolios of listed equity shares, mutual funds, gold jewellery, or commercial plots, long-term capital gains tax can consume substantial wealth. Section 54F of the Income Tax Act provides an avenue to reduce this liability to zero by reinvesting net sale proceeds into a residential house. Understand the crucial differences from Section 54, the proportionate formula, the ₹10 Crore statutory cap, and Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS) mechanics.

Unutilized net sale proceeds must be deposited into an authorized Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS 1988) account prior to the due date of filing ITR under Section 139(1).

Key Takeaways & Executive Summary

  • Eligible Transfers: Any long-term capital asset (stocks, mutual funds, gold, commercial land) other than a residential house property.
  • Reinvestment Benchmark: Reinvest the Net Sale Consideration (entire sale proceeds minus transfer expenses), not merely the capital gains.
  • Proportionate Exemption: If partial net proceeds are invested, exemption is restricted proportionally based on the formula: Capital Gains × (Cost of New House / Net Consideration).
  • The One-House Restriction: The taxpayer must not own more than one residential house on the date of original asset transfer.
  • Statutory Limit: Exemption is capped against a maximum new house cost of ₹10 Crore under Section 54F(1) proviso.

1. What is Section 54F & Why Was It Introduced?

Under the Income Tax Act, 1961, when individuals or Hindu Undivided Families (HUFs) transfer capital assets held for more than the statutory holding period, Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) tax is triggered. With recent legislative amendments fixing equity LTCG at 12.5% above ₹1.25 Lakh and other assets at flat rates, large portfolio liquidations can generate massive tax bills.

Section 54F was enacted by the Parliament of India with a specific policy objective: to stimulate domestic housing investment by encouraging individuals to deploy capital from non-housing assets into residential accommodation. Unlike general investment deductions, Section 54F allows an investor to completely neutralize their capital gains tax on the sale of assets such as equities, mutual funds, physical gold, artwork, or commercial properties, provided the proceeds fund the purchase or construction of a residential home.

2. Eligible Original Assets & Qualified Assessees

Not every entity or asset qualifies for relief under Section 54F. The statute defines eligibility with rigorous legal boundaries:

Parameter Permitted under Section 54F Excluded from Section 54F
Eligible Taxpayers Individuals and Hindu Undivided Families (HUFs) only Companies, Partnership Firms, LLPs, and AOP/BOI
Original Asset Transferred Any Long-Term Capital Asset: Equity Shares, Mutual Funds, Gold, Land, Commercial Property, Patents A Residential House Property (covered under Section 54 instead) or any Short-Term Asset
New Asset Acquired One Residential House situated in India Commercial shops, vacant land without construction, overseas residential properties

3. Section 54 vs Section 54F: The Crucial Structural Difference

The most common and dangerous misunderstanding among Indian retail investors is confusing Section 54 with Section 54F. While both provide capital gains exemptions for investing in a residential house, their financial mechanics are fundamentally different:

Section 54: Selling a Residential House

Under Section 54, you only need to reinvest the Capital Gain amount into the new house to claim 100% tax exemption. You are free to keep or spend the remaining principal cost of the old house.

Section 54F: Selling Any Other Asset (Shares, Gold, Land)

Under Section 54F, you must reinvest the entire Net Sale Consideration (Gross Sale Value minus selling brokerage/transfer expenses) into the new residential house to secure 100% exemption. If you reinvest only the capital gain portion, you receive only a fraction of the tax exemption.

4. The Proportionate Exemption Formula Explained

When an investor sells mutual funds or shares for ₹1 Crore with capital gains of ₹60 Lakh, but only invests ₹50 Lakh into a new apartment, Section 54F does not allow claiming the full ₹50 Lakh against the capital gains. Instead, the statute mandates a strict proportionate calculation:

Statutory Formula under Section 54F(1)(b):

Exempt Capital Gain = Long-Term Capital Gain × [ Cost of New Residential House / Net Sale Consideration ]

Where:

  • Cost of New Residential House: Actual capital deployed in purchasing or constructing the house (capped at ₹10 Crore).
  • Net Sale Consideration: Full value of consideration received minus expenses incurred wholly and exclusively in connection with the transfer (brokerage, exchange charges, stamp fees).
  • Taxable Capital Gain: Total LTCG minus Exempt Capital Gain.

5. Statutory Timelines: Purchase vs Construction Windows

The Income Tax Act prescribes rigid statutory timelines for acquiring the new residential accommodation. Missing these deadlines by even a single day results in full disallowance of the exemption:

Mode of Acquisition Permitted Statutory Window Reference Benchmark
Prior Purchase Within 1 year before the date of transfer Date of original asset sale
Subsequent Purchase Within 2 years after the date of transfer Date of original asset sale
Construction of House Within 3 years after the date of transfer Date of original asset sale

For under-construction flats booked with a builder, Indian judicial precedents (including CBDT Circular No. 672) view the allotment letter or builder-buyer agreement as construction, granting the 3-year completion window.

6. The ₹10 Crore Statutory Ceiling (Budget 2023 Amendment)

Historically, Ultra-High-Net-Worth Individuals (UHNIs) used Section 54F to shield hundreds of crores of gains from startup equity buybacks and real estate transactions by acquiring ultra-luxury mansions. To curb this practice, Finance Act 2023 introduced a statutory ceiling of ₹10 Crore on Section 54F (and Section 54) effective from FY 2023-24 (AY 2024-25 onwards).

Under the amended proviso to Section 54F(1), where the cost of the new residential property exceeds ₹10 Crore, the amount exceeding ₹10 Crore is completely ignored for calculating the exemption. The maximum investment recognized in the formula is legally restricted to ₹10,00,00,000.

7. The Property Ownership Test: The 1-House Limitation

Section 54F contains strict anti-avoidance covenants concerning property ownership. You are disqualified from claiming Section 54F if, on the date of the transfer of the original asset:

  1. You own more than one residential house (other than the new house). If you already own two flats, you cannot claim Section 54F on mutual fund sales.
  2. You purchase any residential house (other than the new house) within a period of 1 year after the date of transfer.
  3. You construct any residential house (other than the new house) within a period of 3 years after the date of transfer.

Commercial shops, vacant land, or industrial godowns owned by the assessee do not count as residential houses and do not trigger disqualification.

8. Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS) Deposit Rules

If you sell your mutual fund portfolio or gold in November 2026, the statutory due date for filing your Income Tax Return for FY 2026-27 is July 31, 2027 (for non-audit assessees). If you have not utilized the entire net sale consideration towards buying or constructing the new house by July 31, 2027, you must deposit the unutilized balance into the Capital Gains Account Scheme (CGAS 1988) at an authorized public sector bank branch before filing your ITR.

  • Type A (Savings Account): Liquid savings deposit paying regular bank interest. Suitable if you are withdrawing funds frequently for ongoing builder installments.
  • Type B (Term Deposit): Fixed deposit offering higher interest rates. Suitable for funds to be deployed after 12 to 24 months.

Failing to deposit the unutilized net consideration in CGAS prior to the Section 139(1) ITR filing due date makes the uninvested capital gains immediately taxable in the relevant assessment year.

9. Practical Worked Numerical Case Study: Equity Portfolio Sale

Let us examine a real-world case study comparing full reinvestment versus partial reinvestment under the proportionate formula.

Investor Profile: Ananya

  • Asset Sold: Multi-cap equity mutual funds held for over 5 years.
  • Sale Value: ₹80,00,000
  • Expenses of Transfer: ₹40,000 (Brokerage and transaction charges)
  • Net Sale Consideration: ₹80,00,000 − ₹40,000 = ₹79,60,000
  • Indexed / Acquisition Cost: ₹25,00,000
  • Long-Term Capital Gain: ₹79,60,000 − ₹25,00,000 = ₹54,60,000
  • Existing Residential Property Owned: 1 residential apartment in Mumbai.

Scenario 1: Full Reinvestment in New Residential House

Ananya purchases a new apartment for ₹82,00,000 within 18 months of sale.

  • Amount Invested (₹82,00,000) ≥ Net Consideration (₹79,60,000).
  • Exemption under Section 54F: 100% of LTCG = ₹54,60,000.
  • Taxable Capital Gain: ₹0 (Full tax saved).

Scenario 2: Partial Reinvestment

Ananya purchases a smaller apartment for ₹50,00,000 and keeps the remaining funds in a bank FD.

Applying the proportionate formula:

Exempt LTCG = ₹54,60,000 × (₹50,00,000 / ₹79,60,000) = ₹34,29,648

  • Exempt Capital Gain: ₹34,29,648
  • Taxable Capital Gain: ₹54,60,000 − ₹34,29,648 = ₹20,30,352

Ananya pays 12.5% tax on the taxable surplus of ₹20,30,352 (subject to Section 112A threshold mechanics if applicable), saving tax on over ₹34 Lakh of gains.

10. 3-Year Lock-In & Circumstances Triggering Exemption Revocation

Section 54F is subject to a statutory 3-year lock-in period. The exemption granted will be revoked and treated as long-term capital gains in the year of default if any of the following occur:

  • Sale of New House Within 3 Years: If you transfer or sell the newly acquired residential house within 3 years of purchase or construction, the entire exempted capital gain is deemed to be long-term capital gains in the financial year in which the house is sold.
  • Purchase of an Additional Residential House: If you purchase any second residential house within 1 year from the original asset sale date, the exemption is cancelled.
  • Construction of an Additional Residential House: If you construct another residential house within 3 years from the original asset sale date, the entire exemption is revoked.

11. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Can I claim Section 54F if I jointly purchase the new house with my spouse?

Yes. Various High Courts (including the Delhi High Court in CIT v. Ravinder Kumar Arora) have ruled that investing sale proceeds in the joint names of the assessee and their spouse does not disqualify the exemption, provided the entire funds were contributed by the assessee who sold the original asset.

Can I claim both Section 54EC and Section 54F on the same sale?

Yes, provided you satisfy the conditions for both. If you sell commercial land, you can deploy part of the proceeds into Section 54EC bonds (up to ₹50 Lakh) and the remaining net consideration into a residential house under Section 54F, optimizing tax across both provisions.

Can Section 54F be claimed under the New Tax Regime (Section 115BAC)?

Yes. Section 54F is an exemption under Chapter IV (Computation of Total Income under the head Capital Gains), not a Chapter VI-A itemized deduction. It remains fully applicable under both the Old Tax Regime and the New Tax Regime.

Can I buy a residential plot of land under Section 54F?

Buying a vacant residential plot alone does not qualify for Section 54F exemption. However, if you purchase a plot and complete the construction of a residential house on it within the statutory 3-year window, the combined cost of the plot and the construction qualifies for exemption.
