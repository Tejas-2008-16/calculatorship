What is NPS (National Pension System)? A Complete Guide for Indian Salaried Employees

Written by Tejas Waghmare  •  Published: 26 July 2026  •  Last reviewed: 23 November 2026  •  Tax Law: Sections 80CCD(1), 80CCD(1B), 80CCD(2) & 10(12A)

Educational pension guide only. Not personal investment or tax advice. National Pension System rules and tax exemptions are regulated by PFRDA and the Ministry of Finance. Read our full disclaimer.

The National Pension System (NPS) is a government-regulated, low-cost market-linked retirement scheme managed by PFRDA. It offers an exclusive ₹50,000 annual tax deduction under Section 80CCD(1B) beyond the 80C limit, plus up to 14% employer deduction under Section 80CCD(2). At age 60, 60% of the accumulated corpus is 100% tax-free under Section 10(12A), while the remaining 40% purchases a monthly pension annuity.

Because retirement planning in India demands balancing immediate tax relief under Section 80CCD against the mandatory forty-percent annuity purchase at age sixty, salaried professionals must weigh whether locking their capital into a long-term pension vehicle aligns with their broader lifestyle goals. Imagine you earn Rs 14 Lakh a year. Salaried earners already exhausting their Rs 1.5 Lakh Section 80C limit through EPF and mutual fund ELSS still face substantial tax bills. Then a colleague mentions an exclusive extra Rs 50,000 tax deduction under Section 80CCD(1B) if you open an NPS account. Naturally, you wonder what the catch is.

Your money is strictly locked until you turn 60, and at retirement, at least 40% must go into a taxable monthly pension annuity. Whether that trade-off makes sense depends on your tax slab and your personal liquidity needs.

Understanding the Basics: How NPS Works

Administered by PFRDA, the National Pension System is a government-backed, market-linked retirement savings scheme regulated by the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA). Originally launched for government employees in 2004 and opened to all Indian citizens in 2009, NPS today allows any citizen aged 18 to 70 to open an account.

Its underlying framework is simple: you contribute regularly throughout your working life, your money is professionally managed across equity and debt assets, and at retirement (age 60) you receive part as a tax-free lump sum and the rest as a monthly pension. Think of it as a long-term market-linked investment with a built-in pension component.

Tier 1 vs Tier 2: What Is the Difference?

When you enroll in NPS, you choose between two account types, though only Tier 1 is mandatory.

NPS Tier 1: The Core Pension Account

This is the primary pension account. Money is locked until age 60, with narrow exceptions for partial withdrawals. Annual contributions require at least Rs 1,000. All genuine tax benefits, including the Rs 1.5 Lakh 80C deduction and the exclusive Rs 50,000 deduction under 80CCD(1B), apply only to Tier 1.

NPS Tier 2: The Flexible Savings Account

Tier 2 is optional, requires an active Tier 1, and has zero lock-in - you can withdraw anytime. Crucially, Tier 2 offers no tax benefits for private-sector employees. It functions essentially as a mutual fund savings account dressed in pension clothing. Most people are better served by a liquid mutual fund for short-term parking of funds.

The Tax Benefit That Makes NPS Worth Considering

NPS has three separate tax layers that stack on top of each other:

  • Section 80CCD(1): NPS contributions count toward your 80C bucket, subject to the ₹1.5 lakh ceiling shared with EPF, PPF, ELSS, and others.
  • Section 80CCD(1B): The special one. An additional ₹50,000 deduction, exclusively for NPS Tier 1 contributions - completely separate from the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit. No other investment instrument gives you this extra slot.
  • Section 80CCD(2): If your employer also contributes to your NPS (up to 10% of basic salary for private employees), that employer contribution is deductible under 80CCD(2) with no upper cap.
Arjun’s Tax Saving: He puts ₹50,000 into NPS Tier 1. At the 30% slab plus 4% cess, he saves roughly ₹15,600 in taxes. That is a 31.2% instant return before his money is even deployed into the market.

How Your NPS Money Is Actually Invested

NPS invests across three asset classes managed by PFRDA-registered Pension Fund Managers (PFMs) - currently including SBI Pension Funds, HDFC Pension Management, ICICI Prudential Pension Funds, and Kotak Mahindra Pension Fund, among others.

  • Asset Class E (Equity): Pure growth. Money goes into Indian equities and index stocks. Cap: 75%.
  • Asset Class C (Corporate Debt): Steady income. Funds invest in high-rated corporate bonds and debentures.
  • Asset Class G (Government Securities): Sovereign backing. Invests in Central and State government securities.

Active Choice vs Auto Choice

Active choice gives you full control over the E/C/G split (equity capped at 75%). Good if you understand asset allocation and will rebalance annually. Auto choice (Lifecycle Fund) automatically reduces equity as you age - aggressive (LC-75) in your 20s, conservative by your mid-50s. For most people, LC-75 Auto choice is a sensible starting point.

Historically, NPS equity funds have broadly delivered approximate returns in the range of 10-14% CAGR over long periods; debt-heavy allocations roughly 7-10%. These are indicative only - past performance does not predict future returns. For current fund-wise return data, check npstrust.org.in.

Maturing at Age 60: Corpus Distribution and Annuities

At age 60, your NPS corpus splits into two mandatory parts:

  • 60% lump sum: You can withdraw up to 60% as a one-time payment. That lump sum is completely tax-free.
  • 40% mandatory annuity: At least 40% must go into an annuity from a PFRDA-empanelled provider (LIC, SBI Life, HDFC Life, etc.). That annuity contract pays a fixed monthly pension for life. Annuity payouts are taxable per your income slab at the time of receipt. Slabs apply each financial year.
The Annuity Consideration: Current annuity rates from most providers are roughly 5-6.5% per year on the corpus. A fixed annuity does not automatically adjust for inflation, which means its real purchasing power erodes over time. Such low annuity yield remains a genuine limitation worth understanding before committing heavily to NPS. Consider how the fitment factor alters gross salary step-by-step: Discipline pays. Rules stay rigid. Start early.

Who Should Actually Use NPS?

Subscribers benefit the most when falling into the 20% or 30% tax brackets with their 80C already maxed out. A disciplined holding period allows equity and corporate debt compounding to unfold without intermediate tax drag. If your employer offers an NPS contribution under 80CCD(2), opting in is almost always worth it: it is essentially extra compensation many employees leave unclaimed simply by not enrolling.

Who Should Skip NPS (Or Use It Selectively)?

Consider limiting your NPS contribution to just the ₹50,000 80CCD(1B) amount - or skipping it altogether - if:

  • You are in your 20s with near-term financial goals: A home down payment, higher education, or a business within the next 10-15 years? Strict lock-in limits will frustrate you because there is no clean exit. Equity mutual funds give you the flexibility NPS cannot.
  • Earner in 5% or nil tax bracket: The tax saving is not large enough to justify locking money away for decades.
  • Investor dislikes mandatory annuities: If the idea of putting 40% of your retirement savings into a 5-6% fixed pension bothers you, a SIP-based mutual fund portfolio with a Systematic Withdrawal Plan at retirement may suit you better. See how SWP works as a retirement income tool.
A Balanced Strategy: Contribute exactly ₹50,000/year to NPS Tier 1 to claim the 80CCD(1B) deduction, and invest the rest in equity mutual funds for flexibility. Doing so captures the full tax relief without over-locking your wealth. Balance matters. Keep flexibility. Check the math. Small fees compound. Stay grounded.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I withdraw from NPS before age 60?
Partial withdrawals from NPS Tier 1 are allowed after 3 years for specific reasons - children’s education, marriage, home purchase, or critical illness - up to 25% of your own contributions. Full premature exit is allowed only after 10 years, and you must buy an annuity with at least 80% of the corpus. Tier 2 has no lock-in whatsoever.
Is the NPS maturity amount taxable?
At age 60, you can withdraw up to 60% of your NPS corpus as a tax-free lump sum. Mandatory annuity rules require that the remaining 40% goes into an annuity plan, and monthly pension payouts from that annuity remain taxable per your income slab at the time you receive it.
What is the extra tax benefit of NPS beyond the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit?
Section 80CCD(1B) gives you an additional ₹50,000 deduction for NPS Tier 1 contributions - completely separate from your ₹1.5 lakh 80C ceiling. At the 30% slab, this saves approximately ₹15,600 annually (including cess). No other investment gives you this exclusive extra slot.
What is the difference between Active and Auto choice in NPS?
Active choice lets you set your own E/C/G allocation with equity capped at 75%. Auto choice automatically reduces equity as you age - aggressive in your 20s, conservative by your mid-50s. Active suits those who want control; Auto is the sensible default for a set-and-forget approach.

How to Open an NPS Account

Opening NPS is simpler than most people expect. Go to the eNPS portal at enps.nsdl.com, use your PAN and Aadhaar-linked mobile number, and upload your bank details - the whole process takes about 20-30 minutes. Your PRAN (Permanent Retirement Account Number) is issued instantly. If your employer is PFRDA-registered, ask HR about enrolling through corporate NPS payroll integration.
