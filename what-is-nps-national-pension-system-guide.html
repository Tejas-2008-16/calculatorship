What is NPS (National Pension System)? A Complete Guide for Indian Salaried Employees

NPS is one of the most tax-efficient retirement tools available to Indian salaried employees — and also one of the most misunderstood. If you have been ignoring it because of the “lock-in until 60” reputation, this guide will help you decide whether that tradeoff is actually worth it for your situation.

Meet Arjun, a 29-year-old software engineer in Pune earning a CTC of ₹14 lakhs. He maxes out his 80C with EPF and ELSS, runs a solid mutual fund SIP, and his CA just flagged that he is still paying ₹32,000 in income tax. A colleague mentioned NPS offers an extra ₹50,000 deduction. Arjun’s first reaction: “That sounds too good to be true. What’s the catch?”

There is a catch — a significant one. Whether it matters for you depends entirely on your age, liquidity needs, and how comfortable you are with locking money away until 60. Let’s work through it honestly.

What Exactly is NPS?

The National Pension System is a government-backed, market-linked retirement savings scheme regulated by the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA). Originally launched for government employees in 2004 and opened to all Indian citizens in 2009, NPS today allows any citizen aged 18 to 70 to open an account.

The structure is simple: you contribute regularly throughout your working life, your money is professionally managed across equity and debt assets, and at retirement (age 60) you receive part as a tax-free lump sum and the rest as a monthly pension. Think of it as a long-term market-linked investment with a built-in pension component.

Tier 1 vs Tier 2: What Is the Difference?

When you open NPS, you get two account types — only Tier 1 is mandatory.

NPS Tier 1 — The Core Pension Account

This is the main account. Money here is locked until age 60, with limited exceptions for partial withdrawals. The minimum annual contribution is ₹1,000. All real tax benefits — both the 80C portion and the exclusive ₹50,000 under 80CCD(1B) — apply only to Tier 1 contributions. This is the account that actually matters for retirement planning.

NPS Tier 2 — The Flexible Savings Account

Tier 2 is optional, requires an active Tier 1, and has zero lock-in — you can withdraw anytime. The catch: Tier 2 offers no tax benefits for private-sector employees. It is essentially a savings account dressed in NPS clothing. Most people are better served by a liquid mutual fund for short-term parking of funds.

The Tax Benefit That Makes NPS Worth Considering

NPS has three separate tax layers that stack on top of each other:

  • Section 80CCD(1): NPS contributions count toward your 80C bucket, subject to the ₹1.5 lakh ceiling shared with EPF, PPF, ELSS, and others.
  • Section 80CCD(1B): The special one. An additional ₹50,000 deduction, exclusively for NPS Tier 1 contributions — completely separate from the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit. No other investment instrument gives you this extra slot.
  • Section 80CCD(2): If your employer also contributes to your NPS (up to 10% of basic salary for private employees), that employer contribution is deductible under 80CCD(2) with no upper cap.
Arjun’s Tax Saving: He puts ₹50,000 into NPS Tier 1. At the 30% slab plus 4% cess, he saves roughly ₹15,600 in taxes. That is a 31.2% instant return before his money is even deployed into the market.

How Your NPS Money Is Actually Invested

NPS invests across three asset classes managed by PFRDA-registered Pension Fund Managers (PFMs) — currently including SBI Pension Funds, HDFC Pension Management, ICICI Prudential Pension Funds, and Kotak Mahindra Pension Fund, among others.

  • Asset Class E (Equity): Stocks and equity-related instruments. Maximum allowed allocation: 75%.
  • Asset Class C (Corporate Bonds): Fixed-income from corporates. Moderate returns, lower risk than equity.
  • Asset Class G (Government Securities): Govt bonds. Lowest risk, adds stability to the portfolio.

Active Choice vs Auto Choice

Active choice gives you full control over the E/C/G split (equity capped at 75%). Good if you understand asset allocation and will rebalance annually. Auto choice (Lifecycle Fund) automatically reduces equity as you age — aggressive (LC-75) in your 20s, conservative by your mid-50s. For most people, LC-75 Auto choice is a sensible starting point.

Historically, NPS equity funds have broadly delivered approximate returns in the range of 10–14% CAGR over long periods; debt-heavy allocations roughly 7–10%. These are indicative only — past performance does not predict future returns. For current fund-wise return data, check npstrust.org.in.

What Happens When You Turn 60?

At age 60, your NPS corpus splits into two mandatory parts:

  • 60% lump sum: You can withdraw up to 60% as a one-time payment. This is completely tax-free.
  • 40% mandatory annuity: At least 40% must go into an annuity from a PFRDA-empanelled provider (LIC, SBI Life, HDFC Life, etc.). This pays a fixed monthly pension for life. The pension income is taxable per your slab at the time of receipt.
The Annuity Consideration: Current annuity rates from most providers are roughly 5–6.5% per year on the corpus. A fixed annuity does not automatically adjust for inflation, which means its real purchasing power erodes over time. This is a genuine limitation worth understanding before committing heavily to NPS.

Who Should Actually Use NPS?

NPS makes the most sense if you are in the 20% or 30% tax slab with your 80C already maxed out. It works well if you genuinely will not need the money before 60 — NPS rewards long-term patience. If your employer offers an NPS contribution under 80CCD(2), opting in is almost always worth it: it is essentially extra compensation many employees leave unclaimed simply by not enrolling.

Who Should Skip NPS (Or Use It Selectively)?

Consider limiting your NPS contribution to just the ₹50,000 80CCD(1B) amount — or skipping it altogether — if:

  • You are in your 20s with near-term financial goals: A home down payment, higher education, or a business within the next 10–15 years? The lock-in will frustrate you. Equity mutual funds give you the flexibility NPS cannot.
  • You are in the 5% or nil tax bracket: The tax saving is not large enough to justify locking money away for decades.
  • You dislike the mandatory annuity structure: If the idea of putting 40% of your retirement savings into a 5–6% fixed pension bothers you, a SIP-based mutual fund portfolio with a Systematic Withdrawal Plan at retirement may suit you better. See how SWP works as a retirement income tool.
The Balanced Approach: Contribute exactly ₹50,000/year to NPS Tier 1 to claim the 80CCD(1B) deduction, and invest the rest in equity mutual funds for flexibility. You get the full tax benefit without over-locking your wealth.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I withdraw from NPS before age 60?
Partial withdrawals from NPS Tier 1 are allowed after 3 years for specific reasons — children’s education, marriage, home purchase, or critical illness — up to 25% of your own contributions. Full premature exit is allowed only after 10 years, and you must buy an annuity with at least 80% of the corpus. Tier 2 has no lock-in whatsoever.
Is the NPS maturity amount taxable?
At age 60, you can withdraw up to 60% of your NPS corpus as a tax-free lump sum. The remaining 40% must go into an annuity plan, and the monthly pension income from that annuity is taxable per your income slab at the time you receive it.
What is the extra tax benefit of NPS beyond the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit?
Section 80CCD(1B) gives you an additional ₹50,000 deduction for NPS Tier 1 contributions — completely separate from your ₹1.5 lakh 80C ceiling. At the 30% slab, this saves approximately ₹15,600 annually (including cess). No other investment gives you this exclusive extra slot.
What is the difference between Active and Auto choice in NPS?
Active choice lets you set your own E/C/G allocation with equity capped at 75%. Auto choice automatically reduces equity as you age — aggressive in your 20s, conservative by your mid-50s. Active suits those who want control; Auto is the sensible default for a set-and-forget approach.

How to Open an NPS Account

Opening NPS is simpler than most people expect. Go to the eNPS portal at enps.nsdl.com, use your PAN and Aadhaar-linked mobile number, and upload your bank details — the whole process takes about 20–30 minutes. Your PRAN (Permanent Retirement Account Number) is issued instantly. If your employer is PFRDA-registered, ask HR about enrolling through corporate NPS payroll integration.
