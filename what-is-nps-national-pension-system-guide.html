What is NPS (National Pension System)? PFRDA Regulations, Tax Provisions & Mechanics

Hi, I'm Tejas — an engineering student researching personal finance. This guide analyzes the National Pension System (NPS) architecture under Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA) regulations and Income Tax Act provisions (Sections 80CCD(1), 80CCD(1B), and 80CCD(2)). It is written with AI assistance and checked against official circulars. It is purely educational and does not constitute investment or tax advice.

The National Pension System is an individual retirement savings vehicle administered by PFRDA. It combines professional fund management across equity and debt with specific income tax deduction tiers. For salaried and self-employed taxpayers navigating Indian retirement options, the National Pension System (NPS) represents a unique statutory structure. Unlike voluntary mutual funds or fixed-rate small savings schemes, NPS integrates defined regulatory lock-ins until age 60 with tiered tax deductions and compulsory life annuity purchase requirements.

What is the National Pension System?

The National Pension System is a market-linked defined-contribution pension framework established by the Government of India and regulated by the Pension Fund Regulatory and Development Authority (PFRDA). Originally introduced for Central Government employees joining service on or after January 1, 2004, it was expanded in 2009 to all Indian citizens aged between 18 and 70 on a voluntary basis.

Under this system, each subscriber is assigned a unique 12-digit Permanent Retirement Account Number (PRAN) administered by Central Recordkeeping Agencies (CRAs like Protean, KFintech, or Computer Age Management Services). Contributions are pooled and managed by PFRDA-registered Pension Fund Managers (PFMs).

Tier-I vs Tier-II Account Architecture

NPS operates under a two-tiered account framework:

Tier-I: Mandatory Pension Account

The Tier-I account represents the core retirement vehicle. Withdrawals are strictly restricted until age 60, with limited exceptions under PFRDA exit regulations for designated emergencies (such as medical treatment, higher education, or residential house construction after a minimum 3-year holding period, capped at 25% of self-contributions). All statutory income tax deductions apply exclusively to Tier-I contributions.

Tier-II: Voluntary Investment Account

A Tier-II account is an optional savings facility requiring an active Tier-I PRAN. It permits unrestricted subscriptions and redemptions with zero lock-in. However, for private-sector subscribers, contributions to Tier-II do not qualify for deductions under Chapter VI-A of the Income Tax Act, and capital gains upon withdrawal are taxed according to applicable mutual fund or debt tax classifications.

Statutory Tax Provisions under the Income Tax Act

Tax benefits associated with NPS Tier-I are codified under three separate subsections of Section 80CCD:

  • Section 80CCD(1): Individual employee or self-employed contributions up to 10% of salary (basic + DA) or 20% of gross total income for non-salaried individuals, subject to the overall ₹1,50,000 ceiling under Section 80CCE (shared with PPF, EPF, ELSS).
  • Section 80CCD(1B): An exclusive additional deduction of up to ₹50,000 for contributions to Tier-I, over and above the ₹1,50,000 ceiling of Section 80CCE. Under the Old Tax Regime, this allows a total Chapter VI-A retirement deduction of up to ₹2,00,000.
  • Section 80CCD(2): Employer contributions on behalf of an employee up to 10% of salary (or 14% for Central/State Government employees). Under Section 115BAC, this deduction remains available under both the Old and New Tax Regimes, subject to the aggregate ₹7,50,000 annual limit on employer contributions to EPF, NPS, and approved superannuation funds under Section 17(2)(vii).

Asset Classes and Portfolio Management Options

Subscribers allocate capital across four asset classes regulated by PFRDA:

  • Asset Class E (Equity): Equities and related instruments, capped at a maximum of 75% for private subscribers up to age 50, gradually tapering thereafter under Active Choice.
  • Asset Class C (Corporate Debt): Corporate bonds, debentures, and infrastructure debt.
  • Asset Class G (Government Securities): Central and State Government securities and Treasury bills.
  • Asset Class A (Alternative Assets): Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs), REITs, and InvITs (capped at 5%).

Subscribers choose between Active Choice (where the individual manually sets the asset split) and Auto Choice (Lifecycle Funds), where asset allocation automatically shifts from equity to debt as age advances (Aggressive LC-75, Moderate LC-50, or Conservative LC-25).

PFRDA Exit and Annuity Regulations at Age 60

Upon attaining 60 years of age (or normal superannuation), the accumulated PRAN corpus is governed by PFRDA (Exits and Withdrawals) Regulations:

  • Up to 60% Lump Sum Withdrawal: Exempt from income tax under Section 10(12A) of the Income Tax Act.
  • Minimum 40% Mandatory Annuity: Must be deployed with an IRDAI-regulated and PFRDA-empanelled Annuity Service Provider (ASP) to generate a monthly pension. The purchase of the annuity is not taxable; however, the periodic pension received is treated as salary/income from other sources and taxed at the subscriber's applicable slab rates.
  • Corpus Below ₹5 Lakh: Under prevailing rules, if the total accumulated corpus at age 60 is ₹5,00,000 or less, the subscriber may opt for a 100% lump sum withdrawal without mandatory annuity purchase.
  • Premature Exit (Prior to Age 60): Permitted after 10 years of subscription, but requires a minimum of 80% of the corpus to be annuitized, with only 20% available as a lump sum (or 100% lump sum if the total corpus is ₹2.5 lakh or less).

How to Think About This Yourself

  • Liquidity vs Tax Arbitrage: The ₹50,000 deduction under 80CCD(1B) provides immediate income tax relief under the Old Regime, but capital remains committed until age 60. Evaluate liquidity requirements for medium-term goals before allocating beyond the tax-incentivized threshold.
  • Annuity Inflation Drag: Because at least 40% of the corpus must purchase an annuity at age 60, and standard immediate annuities pay fixed non-indexed yields, consider how post-retirement living expenses will withstand purchasing power erosion.
  • Corporate NPS under New Regime: Employer contributions under Section 80CCD(2) represent one of the few Chapter VI-A deductions retained under Section 115BAC (New Tax Regime).

Where to Get Personalised Help

Official circulars, scheme master circulars, and CRA account access portals are maintained at the PFRDA portal and NPS Trust website. For personalized tax planning across regimes or retirement asset allocation, consult a Chartered Accountant (ICAI) or a fee-only SEBI-Registered Investment Adviser (RIA) via the official SEBI directory.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an individual withdraw from NPS before age 60?
Partial withdrawals from Tier-I are permitted after 3 years for specific contingencies (medical treatment, children's higher education/marriage, residential home purchase), capped at 25% of the subscriber's own contributions. Full premature exit requires 80% mandatory annuitization. Tier-II carries no lock-in.
How is the maturity corpus taxed at superannuation?
Under Section 10(12A), up to 60% of the accumulated corpus withdrawn as a lump sum at age 60 is completely tax-free. The remaining 40% (minimum) deployed in an annuity generates taxable pension income treated under applicable tax slabs upon receipt.
What is the deduction limit under Section 80CCD(1B)?
Section 80CCD(1B) allows an exclusive deduction of up to ₹50,000 for individual contributions to NPS Tier-I, independent of the general ₹1,50,000 cap under Section 80CCE.
What is the difference between Active and Auto choice?
Active choice gives the subscriber manual discretion over asset class weights (with equity capped at 75% up to age 50). Auto choice uses lifecycle matrices that systematically scale down equity allocation toward fixed income as the subscriber ages.
