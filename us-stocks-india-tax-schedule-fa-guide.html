Tax on US Stocks in India (FY 2026-27): 24-Month Rule, Schedule FA & Form 67

Investing in Apple, Microsoft, or vested Silicon Valley tech RSUs? Navigating tax on US stocks India is fundamentally different from domestic shares. From the 24-month 12.5% LTCG holding period to mandatory Schedule FA disclosures and ₹10 lakh Black Money Act penalties, here is your complete survival blueprint.

Key Takeaways: Crucial Facts for US Equity Investors

  • 1. 24-Month Long-Term Cutoff: Because foreign equities are unlisted in India, the long-term holding period is 24 months. Gains realized on shares held >24 months are taxed at 12.5% without indexation. Shares held ≤24 months are taxed at your marginal slab rate.
  • 2. Zero ₹1.25 Lakh Exemption: Section 112A's popular ₹1.25 lakh annual exemption does NOT apply to US stocks or global ETFs. Every single rupee of long-term capital gain is taxable from rupee one under Section 112.
  • 3. Mandatory Schedule FA Disclosure: If you hold even a single fraction of a US share, an unvested/vested RSU, or an open USD cash balance in Charles Schwab, Vested, or Indmoney, you MUST report it in Schedule FA of ITR-2 or ITR-3. Using ITR-1 is illegal.
  • 4. ₹10,00,000 Omission Penalty: Under Section 43 of the Black Money Act, 2015, failing to disclose foreign assets in Schedule FA attracts a flat ₹10 lakh penalty, even if all your income was fully tax-paid and legally acquired via LRS.
  • 5. DTAA Double-Tax Relief: US withholding tax of 25% on dividends can be offset against your Indian tax liability by filing Form 67 before you file your ITR.
  • 6. Rule 115 Currency Conversion: You cannot use spot market exchange rates. All buy, sell, and dividend numbers must be converted using the official SBI Telegraphic Transfer (TT) Buying Rate.

The Bangalore Techie's Monday Morning: When RSUs Trigger an IT Notice

Say Rohan, a 29-year-old staff engineer in Bengaluru, works for an American enterprise software giant. Over the past three years, he accumulated $18,000 worth of Restricted Stock Units (RSUs) on an E*TRADE account and invested another $4,000 through an Indian investment app. When filing his annual income tax return in July, he quickly submitted ITR-1 Sahaj like he always had. His salary TDS was accurate, so he expected zero issues.

Three months later, while sipping tea at his desk, a dreaded email from the Income Tax Department arrives: "Defective Return Notice under Section 139(9) — High-Value Foreign Assets Detected in Incompatible ITR Form." Rohan is terrified. He paid all his salary taxes! What went wrong? Honestly, most salaried folks overlook this: the moment you acquire a single fractional share of a foreign company, filing ITR-1 becomes an illegal omission. You enter the world of unlisted asset taxation, cross-border currency conversions, and rigorous Schedule FA scrutiny under the Black Money Act. Here is how the rules actually work.

Why US Stocks Are Taxed as "Unlisted Shares" in India

Short answer: Because foreign equities trade on exchanges outside India (like the NYSE or NASDAQ) where Indian Securities Transaction Tax (STT) is not paid, Indian tax jurisprudence treats them strictly as unlisted shares.

Under Section 2(42A) of the Income-tax Act, 1961, preferential tax rates (such as Section 111A's 20% STCG and Section 112A's 12.5% LTCG with a ₹1.25 lakh annual exemption) are reserved exclusively for domestic securities traded on recognized Indian exchanges (NSE and BSE) that suffer STT. Because your trades in US equities, Apple shares, or Vanguard ETFs bypass the Indian exchequer's STT net, the law strips away domestic equity concessions.

Consequently, US stocks fall into the residual category of "shares of a company not listed on a recognized stock exchange in India." This classification dictates your holding periods, slab rates, loss set-off rules, and reporting schedules.

The 24-Month Rule: LTCG (12.5%) vs STCG (Slab Rate) Explained

Short answer: Foreign stocks require a holding period of more than 24 months to qualify as Long-Term Capital Assets. LTCG is taxed at a flat rate of 12.5% without indexation. STCG (held ≤ 24 months) is taxed at your regular income tax slab rate.

Following the major tax rationalization introduced in recent Union Budgets, holding period tiers across asset classes were simplified. Prior to the amendment, unlisted shares required 36 months to qualify for long-term status. Under the updated provisions:

  • Long-Term Capital Gains (Holding Period > 24 Months): Taxed under Section 112 at a flat 12.5% (plus applicable surcharge and 4% Health & Education Cess). Indexation benefit is completely eliminated. Crucially, the ₹1.25 lakh statutory LTCG exemption under Section 112A does not apply. If you make a long-term capital gain of ₹2,00,000 on Google shares, tax is calculated on the entire ₹2,00,000 from the first rupee.
  • Short-Term Capital Gains (Holding Period ≤ 24 Months): Added directly to your total taxable income and taxed at your applicable slab rate under the Old or New Tax Regime (which can reach 30% plus surcharge and cess, effectively exceeding 39% for high-income earners).

LRS Remittance Rules: Clearing the ₹7 Lakh vs ₹10 Lakh TCS Confusion

Short answer: Under the Liberalised Remittance Scheme (LRS), Indian resident individuals can remit up to $250,000 per financial year. Tax Collected at Source (TCS) on foreign equity investments is 20% on remittances exceeding the statutory threshold, which aligns with our comprehensive LRS guide.

There is frequent confusion between educational remittances, medical remittances, and overseas investments. Under Section 206C(1G) of the Income-tax Act:

  • For outward remittances specifically made for investing in foreign shares, mutual funds, or foreign brokerage deposits, the TCS rate is 0% up to ₹7,00,000 in a financial year, and 20% on any amount exceeding ₹7,00,000.
  • While certain credit card limits and overseas tour package thresholds were discussed around ₹10 lakh in preliminary budget discussions, retail bank outward wire transfers under LRS strictly enforce the ₹7 lakh threshold for equity investments.
  • TCS Is Not an Extra Tax: TCS is an advance tax collected by your authorized dealer bank. It reflects in your Form 26AS and AIS within 60 days. You can adjust this full TCS amount against your advance tax, salary TDS, or claim a 100% refund when filing your annual ITR.

US Dividends: The 25% DTAA Withholding & Form 67 Foreign Tax Credit

Short answer: When an American corporation pays a dividend, the US IRS withholds a flat 25% tax under the India-US DTAA. In India, foreign dividends are taxed at your slab rate, but you can claim credit for the 25% US tax by filing Form 67 before filing your return.

Unlike capital gains (which are taxed exclusively in the country where the investor resides), dividends are subject to source-based taxation under Article 10 of the Double Tax Avoidance Agreement (DTAA) between India and the United States. Here is the exact mechanics:

  1. US Withholding (W-8BEN): When you open a foreign brokerage account, you submit Form W-8BEN confirming you are a tax resident of India. Standard US dividend withholding is 30%, but under the India-US DTAA treaty, this is capped at 25%. If Apple declares a $100 dividend, your broker deposits $75 in your account and remits $25 directly to the US IRS.
  2. Indian Tax Treatment: The gross dividend ($100 converted to INR) must be declared under 'Income from Other Sources' and is taxed at your ordinary slab rate.
  3. Claiming Foreign Tax Credit (FTC): Under Section 90 of the Income-tax Act, you are entitled to claim credit for the 25% tax withheld in the US to eliminate double taxation. To claim this, you MUST electronically file Form 67 on the income tax portal along with a Form 1042-S or foreign broker statement before or on the due date of filing your ITR. You then report the numbers in Schedule FSI (Foreign Source Income) and Schedule TR (Tax Relief) in ITR-2.

Schedule FA (Foreign Assets): The Calendar-Year Trap & ₹10 Lakh Penalty

Short answer: Schedule FA requires reporting every foreign asset held at any point during the relevant calendar year. Failure to report carries a non-negotiable penalty of ₹10,00,000 under Section 43 of the Black Money Act, 2015.

This is where 90% of Indian tech employees and retail investors stumble. Schedule FA does not follow the Indian Financial Year (April to March). It operates on a Calendar Year (1 January to 31 December) accounting basis:

  • What Must Be Reported: Table A3 (Foreign Equity and Debt Interest) requires reporting every foreign stock held, including peak investment value, closing investment value, gross proceeds on sale, and gross dividends received. Table A1 requires reporting any foreign depository cash balance (your uninvested USD account balance in the US broker).
  • Who Must File: Every individual who qualifies as a Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR). Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident (RNOR) individuals are exempt.
  • The ₹10 Lakh Black Money Penalty: Section 43 of the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015 empowers the tax officer to levy a flat penalty of ₹10,00,000 for non-disclosure or inaccurate disclosure in Schedule FA. Even if you made a loss on your shares, or invested just $50 through a mobile app, non-disclosure triggers automated compliance inquiries. While Budget 2024 introduced a compassionate carve-out exempting non-reporting of small foreign bank accounts under ₹5 lakh, equity assets must still be reported scrupulously.

SBI TT Buying Rate: Converting USD Gains to INR Under Rule 115

Short answer: You cannot use Google, Bloomberg, or live market forex rates. Rule 115 of the Income-tax Rules mandates using the State Bank of India (SBI) Telegraphic Transfer (TT) Buying Rate applicable on the last day of the month preceding the transaction.

Under Indian tax law, capital gains must be determined in Indian currency. To ensure standardization across all taxpayers, the Central Board of Direct Taxes (CBDT) established Rule 115:

  • For Capital Gains: You must convert the USD purchase cost using the SBI TT Buying Rate of the last day of the month immediately preceding the month of purchase. You convert the USD sales consideration using the SBI TT Buying Rate of the last day of the month immediately preceding the month of sale.
  • For Dividends: Converted using the SBI TT Buying Rate of the last day of the month immediately preceding the month in which the dividend is declared or paid.

Setting Off US Stock Losses: What Indian Law Permits

Short answer: Short-term capital losses from US stocks can be set off against both STCG and LTCG. Long-term capital losses can be set off only against long-term capital gains. Losses can be carried forward for 8 assessment years if filed on time.

Under Section 70 and Section 71 of the Income-tax Act, the standard loss set-off rules apply to foreign securities:

  • Short-Term Capital Loss (STCL): Can be set off against any short-term capital gain or long-term capital gain (whether from domestic stocks, US stocks, gold, or real estate).
  • Long-Term Capital Loss (LTCL): Can be set off strictly against Long-Term Capital Gains. You cannot set off long-term US stock losses against short-term domestic equity gains.
  • Carry Forward (8 Years): Unabsorbed capital losses can be carried forward for up to 8 consecutive Assessment Years, provided you file your ITR on or before the statutory due date under Section 139(1). Frequently Asked Questions

What is the holding period for Long-Term Capital Gains (LTCG) on US stocks in India?

Under the Income-tax Act, 1961, foreign stocks not listed on recognized Indian stock exchanges are classified as unlisted shares. Following the rationalized Budget 2024/2026 amendments, the holding period threshold for long-term capital gains is 24 months (reduced from the legacy 36 months). Holding for 24 months or less is short-term.

Does the ₹1.25 Lakh Section 112A capital gains exemption apply to US stocks?

No. Section 112A's ₹1.25 lakh annual LTCG exemption applies exclusively to domestic equity shares and equity mutual funds where Securities Transaction Tax (STT) is paid. LTCG on US stocks is governed by Section 112 at a flat 12.5% rate without indexation, with zero basic exemption.

Who is required to file Schedule FA (Foreign Assets) in India?

Every individual who is a Resident and Ordinarily Resident (ROR) in India who holds any foreign equity shares, vested RSUs, ESPPs, or foreign brokerage cash balances at any time during the relevant accounting period (calendar year) must report in Schedule FA. Non-Resident Indians (NRIs) and RNORs are exempt.

What is the penalty for failing to report US stocks in Schedule FA?

Under Section 43 of the Black Money (Undisclosed Foreign Income and Assets) and Imposition of Tax Act, 2015, failure to furnish or inaccurate disclosure of foreign assets carries a flat penalty of ₹10,00,000 (Ten Lakh Rupees), though recent statutory amendments provide relief for minor bank balances below ₹5 lakh.

How does Form 67 help claim credit for US dividend withholding tax?

The US IRS withholds 25% tax on dividend payouts to Indian residents under Article 10 of the India-US DTAA. To prevent double taxation, you file Form 67 along with Schedule FSI and Schedule TR before submitting ITR-2 or ITR-3, claiming Foreign Tax Credit (FTC) under Section 90 against your Indian tax liability.

What currency conversion rate must be used for US stock tax calculations?

Under Rule 115 of the Income-tax Rules, all capital gains, sales proceeds, purchase costs, and foreign dividends must be converted into Indian Rupees using the State Bank of India (SBI) Telegraphic Transfer (TT) Buying Rate applicable on the last day of the month preceding the month in which the income or transaction occurred.

Actionable Next Step: Download Your Foreign Broker Form 1042-S & Trade Ledger

Don't wait for the July ITR filing rush to sort out your foreign assets. Log in to your foreign brokerage account (E*TRADE, Morgan Stanley, Charles Schwab, Indmoney, or Vested) this quarter. Download your detailed trade confirmation statements and Form 1042-S. Match every single buy, sell, and dividend entry against historical SBI TT Buying Rates. When filing your return, always select ITR-2 or ITR-3, submit Form 67 for dividend tax credits, and accurately populate Schedule FA. That proactive discipline ensures your global portfolio stays fully compliant, audit-proof, and penalty-free.
