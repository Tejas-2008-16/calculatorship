How a Home Loan EMI Works: The Math Behind What You Actually Pay the Bank

By tejascopywriter Published: July 2026 Last Updated: July 2026
← Back to Articles

You take a ₹50 lakh home loan for 20 years at 8.5% interest. Your EMI comes out to ₹43,391. You set up the auto-debit, the bank sends you a welcome letter, and you move into the house feeling like you've made it. But here's the part most home loan borrowers never stop to examine: in that very first month, of the ₹43,391 you hand over, roughly ₹35,417 is going straight to the bank as interest. Only ₹7,974 is actually reducing your loan balance.

You're paying ₹43,391 — and only ₹7,974 is making a dent in what you owe. For the first several years of a home loan, you are, in a very real sense, paying rent to the bank for the privilege of calling it your house. This isn't a trick or a conspiracy — it's how all fixed-rate amortising loans work, mathematically. But it has enormous consequences for how you should think about prepayment, tenure choices, and the true cost of your home.

Let's go through the mechanics properly, so you understand exactly what's happening every single month, and what you can do about it.

1. What Is an EMI, Really?

EMI stands for Equated Monthly Instalment. The "equated" part is the key — it means the total payment amount stays the same every month throughout the loan tenure. What changes, month over month, is how that fixed payment is split between paying interest and repaying principal. In the early months, the split is heavily skewed toward interest. As time passes, the principal portion gradually increases and the interest portion decreases. By the final months of the loan, almost your entire EMI is principal repayment because the outstanding loan balance is tiny.

This structure makes EMIs easy to budget for — you know exactly what leaves your account every month. But it creates a counterintuitive reality: even though you've been paying faithfully for years, the outstanding loan balance drops much more slowly in the early years than most borrowers expect. This is precisely why extra payments in the first 5–7 years of a home loan create dramatically more impact than the same extra payment made in Year 15.

2. The EMI Formula: Where the Number Comes From

Your EMI isn't a number the bank makes up — it's a precise mathematical formula. Here it is:

EMI Formula

EMI = P × r × (1 + r)ⁿ ÷ [(1 + r)ⁿ – 1]

Where: P = Principal loan amount | r = Monthly interest rate (Annual Rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100) | n = Total number of monthly instalments

Let's run through it with Rohit and Pooja's situation. They're a couple in Hyderabad who've taken a ₹50 lakh home loan at 8.5% per annum for 20 years. Here's how their EMI is calculated:

  • P = ₹50,00,000
  • Annual rate = 8.5%, so monthly rate r = 8.5 ÷ 12 ÷ 100 = 0.007083
  • n = 20 × 12 = 240 months

Plugging into the formula: EMI = 50,00,000 × 0.007083 × (1.007083)²⁴⁰ ÷ [(1.007083)²⁴⁰ − 1]. The result: ₹43,391 per month. That number is completely fixed for the life of their loan (assuming a fixed rate). Every month, ₹43,391 leaves their account. What changes is the story inside that number.

3. Amortisation: The Most Important Concept Nobody Explains

Amortisation is the gradual reduction of a loan through scheduled payments. What makes it fascinating — and slightly uncomfortable — is seeing how the interest vs. principal split evolves over the life of a loan. Let's walk through Rohit and Pooja's loan at a few key milestones.

In Month 1, the outstanding loan is the full ₹50 lakh. Interest for the month is calculated on ₹50,00,000 at the monthly rate of 0.7083% = ₹35,417. So of their ₹43,391 EMI, only ₹7,974 reduces the principal. In Month 60 (Year 5), after 5 years of faithful payments totalling over ₹26 lakh, their outstanding balance is still around ₹45.8 lakh. The interest component is still roughly ₹32,464 — about 74.8% of their EMI.

Timeline Outstanding Balance Interest in EMI Principal in EMI
Month 1 (Start) ₹50,00,000 ₹35,417 (81.6%) ₹7,974 (18.4%)
Month 60 (Year 5) ₹45,83,000 ₹32,464 (74.8%) ₹10,927 (25.2%)
Month 120 (Year 10) ₹39,70,000 ₹28,121 (64.8%) ₹15,270 (35.2%)
Month 180 (Year 15) ₹30,10,000 ₹21,320 (49.1%) ₹22,071 (50.9%)
Month 210 (Year 17.5) ₹22,50,000 ₹15,937 (36.7%) ₹27,454 (63.3%)
Month 240 (Final) ₹43,084 ₹305 (0.7%) ₹43,086 (99.3%)

Look at that table carefully. The crossover point — where the principal portion finally exceeds the interest portion — happens around Year 15. For the first 15 years of their 20-year loan, Rohit and Pooja are paying more in interest than principal every single month. The bank gets the larger half of their EMI for three quarters of the loan's life.

💡 Key Insight: You Pay More in Interest Than Your Loan Amount On a ₹50 lakh home loan at 8.5% for 20 years, the total amount paid to the bank is: 240 EMIs × ₹43,391 = ₹1,04,13,840. Your principal was ₹50 lakh. The interest paid over 20 years: approximately ₹54.1 lakh — more than the original loan amount itself. Knowing this changes how you think about prepayment — from "nice to have" to "financially critical."

4. Three Strategies That Can Save You Lakhs

Here's the good news: home loan borrowers have more leverage than they realise. Because prepayments reduce the outstanding principal immediately, and future interest is calculated only on the remaining balance, even modest prepayments in the early years create outsized savings.

Strategy 1: Annual Lump-Sum Prepayments

Rohit gets an annual bonus of about ₹1 lakh. If he uses that entire bonus every year as a home loan prepayment — in addition to regular EMIs — a ₹1 lakh prepayment made at the end of Year 1 reduces the outstanding principal immediately, and all future interest is calculated on that lower base. If they do this every year for 10 years (₹10 lakh total in prepayments), their loan that was originally scheduled to run until 2046 gets fully paid off around 2042 — saving them roughly 4 years of EMIs. In rupee terms, that's approximately 48 months × ₹43,391 = ₹20.8 lakh in total EMI payments saved, minus the ₹10 lakh in prepayments made. Net saving: roughly ₹10–11 lakh.

Strategy 2: Slightly Higher Monthly EMI

What if Rohit and Pooja simply paid ₹5,000 extra per month on top of their regular EMI — making their effective monthly payment ₹48,391? That ₹5,000 extra goes entirely toward principal each month, compounding its impact over time. This seemingly modest increase reduces their 20-year loan to approximately 15.5 years — saving them about 4.5 years of tenure and over ₹12–13 lakh in total interest paid over the life of the loan. A small consistent extra payment creates massive leverage.

Strategy 3: Choose a Shorter Tenure Upfront

Tenure Monthly EMI Total Payment Total Interest Paid
20 Years ₹43,391 ₹1,04,13,840 ₹54,13,840
15 Years ₹49,237 ₹88,62,660 ₹38,62,660
Difference +₹5,846/month — Save ₹15.5 lakh

Choosing 15 years over 20 costs you an extra ₹5,846 per month, but saves you ₹15.5 lakh in total interest. If the higher EMI feels genuinely difficult on your cash flow, the 20-year option with aggressive prepayments is your fallback. But if you can manage it, the 15-year loan is dramatically cheaper over its life.

⚠️ Watch Out: A 30-Year Tenure Can Double Your Cost Some banks offer 25 or 30-year home loan tenures, and the lower EMI sounds very attractive. On a ₹50 lakh loan at 8.5% for 30 years, the EMI drops to ₹38,446 — only ₹5,000 less than the 20-year option. But the total interest you pay over 30 years is approximately ₹88 lakh — over 170% of your original loan. You'd pay more in interest than Rohit paid in both principal and interest combined in 20 years.

5. When Does Home Loan Interest Actually Work in Your Favour?

For all the interest that flows out of your account every month, there is a meaningful tax benefit that partially offsets the cost — particularly in the early years when interest payments are highest. Under Section 24(b) of the Income Tax Act, you can claim a deduction of up to ₹2 lakh per year on home loan interest paid on a self-occupied property. In Year 1, Rohit and Pooja are paying approximately ₹4.25 lakh in interest — they can claim the full ₹2 lakh deduction. At 30% bracket, that's ₹62,400 back via tax savings.

Additionally, the principal repayment component of the EMI qualifies for Section 80C deduction — up to the combined ₹1.5 lakh limit across all 80C investments. In the early years, the annual principal repayment is relatively small (under ₹1 lakh), so the entire principal repayment can typically be claimed. Between Section 24 on interest and 80C on principal, Rohit and Pooja can potentially save ₹1.1–1.2 lakh per year in tax at the 30% bracket — meaningfully reducing the effective cost of their home loan, especially in the first decade when interest payments are highest.

6. Floating vs Fixed: One More Thing Worth Understanding

Most home loans issued in India today are on a floating rate — meaning the interest rate changes periodically based on an external benchmark. Since 2019, RBI mandated that all new floating-rate home loans be linked to an external benchmark rate, and most banks have chosen the RBI Repo Rate as their benchmark. This is called RLLR — Repo-Linked Lending Rate. Your home loan rate = Repo Rate + bank's spread.

When RBI cuts the repo rate (as it has done periodically to stimulate the economy), your home loan interest rate should also fall — automatically, under RLLR-linked loans. The reset happens typically every 3 months. This is a major improvement over the old MCLR regime, where banks had discretion over how and when to pass on rate cuts. HDFC, SBI, and ICICI — which together handle over 55% of home loans in India — all offer RLLR-based floating rate products as their primary option.

📊 Did You Know? HDFC, SBI, and ICICI Bank together account for over 55% of all home loans disbursed in India as of 2026. All three transitioned to Repo-Linked Lending Rate (RLLR) pricing for new floating-rate home loans in October 2019, as mandated by RBI — making rate transmission to borrowers faster and more transparent than under the old MCLR system.
✓ Pro Tip: Step Up Your EMI Every Year With Your Salary If your salary increases by 10% annually, try to increase your home loan EMI by at least 5% each year. You won't feel the pain much — the additional amount is absorbed by your income growth — but the cumulative effect on your loan tenure is dramatic. A 5% annual step-up in EMI on a 20-year loan can reduce it to under 13–14 years, saving you lakhs in interest without requiring any lump-sum payments.

Frequently Asked Questions

If I make a prepayment, should I ask the bank to reduce my EMI or reduce my tenure?

Almost always, choose to reduce your tenure. When you reduce the tenure, you pay off the loan faster and save a larger total amount in interest. Reducing the EMI amount instead keeps your financial burden lower each month but extends your time in debt and total interest cost. The only exception is if your monthly cash flow is genuinely strained — in that case, reducing the EMI provides immediate relief, and you can revisit tenure reduction when your income grows.

Does a home loan prepayment attract any penalty in India?

For floating-rate home loans, RBI has prohibited banks and NBFCs from charging any prepayment penalty — so you can make extra payments at any time without additional charges. For fixed-rate home loans, some lenders still charge a prepayment fee (typically 2–3% of the prepaid amount). Always check your loan agreement before making a large prepayment on a fixed-rate loan to understand if any charges apply.

Is it better to invest my surplus money in equity SIPs or use it to prepay my home loan?

This is one of the most common personal finance questions in India — and the honest answer is: it depends on your interest rate. If your home loan rate is 8.5% and equity SIPs historically return 12% over the long run, the math favours investing in equity. But that 12% is not guaranteed — it's a historical average with significant year-to-year variance. The home loan savings of 8.5% are guaranteed and risk-free. A balanced approach — prepay some, invest some — is what most financial planners recommend for most salaried borrowers.

What is the Section 24 home loan interest deduction limit?

Under Section 24(b) of the Income Tax Act, you can deduct up to ₹2 lakh per year on interest paid on a home loan for a self-occupied property. There is no upper limit on deduction for let-out or deemed let-out properties — the actual interest paid can be fully claimed, though the overall loss from house property that can be set off against other income is capped at ₹2 lakh per year.

If interest rates rise, will my EMI go up or will my tenure increase?

On an RLLR-linked floating rate home loan, when the benchmark repo rate rises, banks typically extend your loan tenure first rather than increasing the EMI. If your tenure gets extended beyond the original loan term, the bank may then increase the EMI. Always ask your bank to notify you whenever a rate change affects your loan structure — you have the right to know, and it's worth reviewing annually.
